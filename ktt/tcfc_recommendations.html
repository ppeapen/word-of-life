Summary

The book KNOW GOD DEEPLY, LIVE LIFE BOLDLY is a sincere, Christ-centered, readable devotional book. It is not yet a course. If used as a curriculum for church care cells it will probably inspire people but change few habits.

Strengths

  • There is one clear, memorable idea (Daniel 11:32, Philippians 3:10) that holds the book together.
  • The principle that knowing God is relational, not merely informational, is repeatedly emphasized.
  • The book is Christ-centered throughout. Chapter 3 (Jesus as the fullest revelation) and Chapter 17 (Paul) are strong.
  • The language is plain and illustrations are good (swimming and cricket in Chapter 6, the iceberg in the Introduction).
  • Some chapters present important truths in an honest straight-forward manner (Chapter 9 on cost, Chapter 7 on barriers, Chapter 10’s Hosea word study).

Key weaknesses

  1. It tells learners that information isn't enough, then gives them only information. There are no activities, no assessment, and no guide for those who will lead the discussion.
  2. The aims stated in the Introduction can't be reached by following the material in the book. It's not designed that way. The book promises transformation but offers no mechanism for it.
  3. Chapter 9 can be read as “you won't know God until you've fully surrendered.” What if that isn't true? What if there is a progressive knowledge of God as one progresses in his/her level of commitment?
  4. The book is thin where a Pentecostal course should be strongest. The Spirit’s empowering, resultant boldness (Acts 4), prayer, worship and discernment of the Spirit’s voice are largely missing.
  5. It is under-contextualized for urban Indian young adults. Religious plurality, family pressure, workplace ethics and real persecution are absent. “Pharaoh” and “Babylon” are mentioned but they stay generic.
  6. There is a lot of avoidable repetition and a handful of exegetical slips (table below).
  7. It is not built for untrained lay leaders. There is no timing, no facilitation guidance and no safety guidance for confession-style questions.

Part 1: Overall Evaluation

1.1 Theological assessment

1.1.1 “Who God is” is under-taught

  • This book is titled “Know God.” But it does not systematically present God’s character: His attributes, His Fatherhood, His place in the Trinity. Eleven chapters cover how and why to know God, and six chapters talk about people who did know God. Whatever little is mentioned about the nature of God is scattered. In other words, the book fails to provide even information (what is wrongly called "head knowledge") to the learner.

    I am not saying that this course must present God's atttributes and character in a condensed, systematic way or in a highly technical way as a textbook of Systematic Theology might present it. There are dozens of Scripture passages and stories that can help learners to discover the attributes of God. Such passages could have formed the basis of several lessons. By a careful study of those lessons – from God's Word – learners will come to know the true nature of God. And that is a completely legitimate, albeit incomplete, way of knowing God.

    Given below is a list of some more omissions. (I am not saying that these verses be crammed into the existing book. When the book is redesigned as a collection of structured lessons, you can certainly consider these passages).

    • One of the most glaring omissions is that of the most crucial passage: Jeremiah 9:23-24. Thus says the Lord: “Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, let not the mighty man boast in his might, let not the rich man boast in his riches, but let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me, that I am the Lord who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth. For in these things I delight, declares the Lord.” Not only does this passage tell us how valuable knowledge of God is, but it also talks about the attributes of God that we must personally experience: His steadfast love (hesed), justice, and righteousness.
    • Another omission? Exodus 34:6-7. This is where God introduced Himself to Moses as the Yahweh, the merciful one, gracious, and slow to anger! This could have been included in your chapter 12.
    • Job 42:5 is missing. It is the sharpest text on the book's central theme, hearing about God versus knowing Him, and it comes out of suffering. (Cf. Your chapters 1 and 9)
    • Knowing God is a two-way street. God knows us. Long before we ever knew about Him. And God refuses to acknowledge some people even when they claim to know Him. John 10:14-15; Galatians 4:9; 1 Corinthians 8:3 (being known by God). Matthew 7:21-23 ("I never knew you")
    • Jeremiah 22:15-16 (“Is not this to know me?”). God defines “knowing Him” as a set of actions—such as defending the poor and needy. In our context, that would include the refugees, trafficked women and children, political prisoners, etc. Chapter 1 of your book says knowing God is “moral,” but no concrete biblical content was included there to back it up.
    • Ephesians 3:14-21 (to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge): The great Pauline prayer about knowing God, and it is Spirit-centered (“strengthened with power through his Spirit”). It would also fix the thin Pentecostal content (as pointed out later in this report).
    • 1 Corinthians 1:18-31 (the world did not know God through its philosophy or earthly wisdom): Your Chapter 6 covers “rational knowing” without any mention of 1 Cor 1 where we see the limits of human knowledge in the absence of divine revelation.
    • 2 Corinthians 4:6; John 1:18; John 14:7, 9. These verses tell us where we get to see the glory of God—in the face of Jesus Christ. Your Chapter 3 quotes John 1:14 but stops short of 1:18.
    • 1 Samuel 3:7; 2:12: Samuel served in the temple and “did not yet know Yahweh.” He came to know Yahweh by listening to His words, His judgments. Eli's sons were priests who “did not know Yahweh.” These passages offer a vivid picture of religious activity without relationship.
    • 1 John 4:7-8 ("whoever does not love does not know God") and Titus 1:16 (professing to know Him while denying Him by actions).
    • Isaiah 40:12-31 and 43:10 give God's incomparability or uniqueness and its purpose: “that you may know and believe me.”
    • Ezekiel's repeated phrase “then you will know that I am ” (about 70 times) shows that God’s acts, including judgment, are aimed at being known.
  • The gospel is assumed. There is no plain statement of how a person enters relationship (grace, repentance, faith, assurance). If any learner is a nominal member or seeker, this course has no door for them.

1.1.2 Use of Scripture

The book is mostly faithful to the context of Scripture verses cited. Exposition of scripture passages is done well: Hosea 6:3–6 (yada, hesed, Jesus quoting it), Chapter 11’s historical setting, and Paul’s thorn and “more than conquerors in all these things” in Chapter 17.

The weakness, however, is a pattern of verse-clustering.

  • John 17:3 is quoted in Chapters 1, 2, 6 and 8.
  • John 5:39 appears in Chapters 1, 2 and 5.
  • Philippians 3:10 appears in the Introduction and Chapters 6, 9 and 17.
  • John 14:21 is used nearly identically in Chapters 9 and 10.
  • Hosea 6:3 appears in Chapters 7, 9 and 10.

Repetition teaches nothing new, and learners will feel they've already done this chapter.

1.1.3 Specific accuracy and interpretation issues

Where Issue Fix
Ch. 2 §1 The burning bush and pillar of cloud are filed under “creation and nature.” They are theophanies or miraculous signs. Angelic encounters (§3) and theophanies (§4) also overlap, and Joseph’s experience in Matthew 1:20 was a dream. Re-sort: creation / dreams and visions / angel of the Lord / theophany / Word / Son.
Ch. 4 The Pascal “God-shaped vacuum” line is a popular paraphrase, not Pascal’s wording. Say “often attributed to” or drop it.
Ch. 6 The fourfold typology (rational, practical, relational, intuitive) is unsourced and not biblical. “Intuitive knowing” blends gut feeling with the Spirit’s voice, and the only safeguard is one sentence (”must be tested by Scripture”). Find and cite the real source, and add concrete ways to test what's felt as the Spirit's voice (Acts 17:11; 1 Thess 5:19–21; 1 Cor 14:29).
Ch. 8 “Only those who know God will experience eternal life in its fullest sense” blurs salvation with degrees of intimacy. The Psalm 91 section could be read as “protection-for-the-faithful” message. Clarify; balance with Habakkuk 3:17–18 or Job.
Ch. 9 “Until we have come to this place we will not know God” and “God wants all of us or none of us” make relationship sound earned. Grace is almost absent until Chapter 17. Lines like “dying in the Church of hypothermia” and “Christ without cross, salvation without surrender…” read like unattributed revival-preaching quotations. Anchor surrender in mercy (Rom 12:1 begins “in view of God’s mercy”; Gal 2:20 “who loved me”). Distinguish entering relationship from deepening it. Acknowledge the sources of the quotations or sayings.
Ch. 11 The Maccabees are “people who knew their God,” yet the “little help” of v.34 is often read as the Maccabean revolt, distinct from the “wise” who teach and stumble. The sharp divide “merely know about God → fall away” is pastorally risky. Peter, Elijah and David faltered. A believer who crumbles under pressure may conclude they never knew God. Add nuance and use the faltering-but-restored examples.
Ch. 12 The title “Faced Pharaoh Fearlessly” contradicts Exodus 3–4 (Moses objects five times), and the chapter itself later says Moses “was not fearless.” Exodus 6:3 shows Moses knowing God more after obeying, not before. Retitle, and show the spiral (see §1C).
Ch. 13 Daniel 11:32 is called “the key verse that summarizes Daniel’s life,” but it describes the faithful under Antiochus Epiphanes who came after Daniel. “He stood before kings because he first knelt before God” repeats Ch. 12’s formula almost word for word. Reword and vary.
Ch. 14 “Mordecai taught her the faith” and “never forgot her identity” are inferences. “Knew God deeply” is argued from silence, and the book never mentions God. The palace “selection” is ethically complex and goes unmentioned. Present as inference and let learners wrestle with it.
Ch. 15 “The book of Judges repeatedly describes that period” with the “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” refrain. That refrain appears at 17:6–21:25, after Deborah’s era. The bullet “Cultural expectations and limitations” is never developed, though women’s leadership will certainly come up. Correct, and give care cell leaders a short guidance note.
Ch. 16 Several inferences are stated as fact (”believed that God would … vindicate her”). Mark inferences, and add a brief note.
Ch. 17 The strongest chapter. Minor: “Some instances (Acts 9:23–25; 14:5–7; 22:25; 25:10–12)” is an incomplete sentence. Explain each reference in a line.

1.1.4 Pentecostal distinctives

This is the book’s biggest theological gap relative to its audience.

  • The book’s key word is boldness, but the Pentecostal text on boldness, Acts 4:13, 29–31 (parrhesia, “filled with the Spirit… spoke the word with boldness”), appears only once, in Chapter 8, and only for “wisdom.”
  • The Spirit appears as illuminator (Chapters 5, 10) but not as empowerer. Spirit baptism, gifts, prophecy and healing are untouched.
  • Romans 8:15–16, 26 (the Spirit who makes us cry “Abba”) is the most direct text on relational knowing and is missing.
  • Chapter 5’s “pathways” omit prayer and worship altogether (they appear elsewhere only in passing). The emphasis on “solitude and silence” comes from the contemplative tradition. That is fine, but it needs bridging to Pentecostal practice (tarrying, waiting on the Lord, praying).

1.1.5 Application to learners' context

  • Applications are universal (“What distracts you?”, “Who is your Pharaoh?”) without addressing the specifics faced by young urban Indians.
  • Nothing addresses religious plurality (Chapter 3 says “Salvation is not found through religion” but never equips learners to say it to a Hindu or Muslim colleague or relative)
  • Real problems such as family pressure and marriage decisions, workplace corruption, job insecurity, anti-conversion tensions, or social media aren’t addressed.
  • The “secret place” in Chapter 9 – unless it is used in the figurative sense – ignores that many learners share rooms with family. It’s virtually impossible for many to find a quiet spot.

1.1.6 Barriers and suffering

  • Chapter 7 treats “barriers” as moral failures only: sin, pride, idolatry, unbelief, bitterness. It omits grief, trauma, unanswered prayer, church hurt, and intellectual doubt.
  • The sentence “the obstacle is often within us” can wound some believers. What if the obstacle was caused by others?
  • “Un-forgiveness” is taught from Matthew 6:14 alone, with no guidance for people who have faced abusive situations.

1.2 Pedagogical assessment

Criterion Finding
Learning outcomes Not a single chapter has a set of Learning Objectives. The Introduction’s aims (“look more closely,” “be encouraged”) are not measurable.
Sequence and pacing There are eleven abstract chapters before the first narrative. Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 10 overlap heavily. Chapter length and density vary widely (Chapter 7 and 11 are very dense).
Level fit Language is accessible. Concepts like yada and hesed are fine. Content volume per week is too high for a 90-minute discussion if it is also read aloud. Each chapter may have to be redesigned as proper Lessons that can be covered in 40 to 60 minutes.
Methods About 90% of the book is exposition, with 5–7 discussion questions at the end of each chapter (roughly 95 total). The book never says whether chapters are read at home or read aloud.
Question quality Many are introspective self-audits (“What excuse am I making?”), yes/no, or rhetorical (“Now that you know the ways to know God, will you actively pursue Him?”). Only three ask for a concrete step “this week” (Chapters 1, 10, 15). Nothing sends learners back into the biblical text.
Bloom’s levels [Help 🎞️] The book is almost entirely on the level of “Remember and Understand”, plus personal Reflection. That’s just the basic level in education/training. There is hardly anything that asks learners to apply, analyze, evaluate and create.
Assessment None. No baseline, no check-ins, no evidence that anything changed.
Facilitator support If these care cells are to be led by untrained lay people with zero training in education, they are left with no help. There is no suggestion as to how to time the lessons or activities, no facilitation tips, answer guides, safety guidance, or help for sensitive disclosures.
Backward design “Backward design” is an instructional planning framework that starts with the end goal (desired student learning outcomes) before creating assessments and daily lessons. Such a design is not evident in this book. The content appears to have been written first.
Production quality Typos (“Saviur,” “it’s,” “Means their relationship…,” “((John 5:39–40),” “a not easy path”), duplicated “Table of Contents,” broken ESV hyperlink artifacts in the Introduction, inconsistent Bible versions (ESV, NIV, CEV) with none stated, and two different titles (cover: Know God Deeply, Live Life Boldly; Introduction: Knowing God Deeper and Facing Life Boldly). The creator of the document has not used any built-in style in MS-Word. As a result, the entire document lacks proper structure and formatting.

1.3 Objectives versus Outcomes

1.3.1 Stated aims (as given in the Introduction)

  1. Help disciples “look more closely at the God revealed in Scripture.”
  2. “Learn from the examples of those who walked with Him before us.”
  3. Be “encouraged that we too can live with boldness, hope, and faith.”
  4. Closing promises: “not just a study of ideas, but a doorway into a deeper experience of God,” “transformed from within,” “a deeper love for God and a life that reflects His glory.”

1.3.2 Can the design of this book help learners achieve these goals?

  • Aims 2 and 3 (as encouragement): yes. Part II does this well.
  • Aim 1: only partly. Chapters 2–3 show God’s self-disclosure, but his character is never taught systematically.
  • Aim 4 (transformation, love, “deeper experience”): there is no evidence of intentional training incorporated into this book to ensure any such experience. Transformation requires practice, repetition, feedback and community. Only Chapter 5 lists practices, and none is taught or rehearsed.

1.3.3 Information versus transformation

The book states the diagnosis in almost every chapter (“Knowing God is not simply accumulating knowledge about Him”). The sixth chapter’s swimming illustration is the perfect picture of the book’s own flaw: learners read about the water and never get in.

My honest prediction about what this book will achieve is this: the book will inform and inspire. There will be a motivational “lift” in the room, a good discussion, but hardly any change in daily habits as the weeks go by. The ‘Bible Stories’ chapters may produce more transformation than Part I because narrative invites identification, but even there nothing asks learners to do anything.

1.3.4 A deeper design flaw: the causal arrow

The thesis of this book runs in a linear way:

know God → boldness.

But Scripture reveals a spiral path that grows bigger and bigger:

obey in fear → see God act → know Him more → obey more boldly.

Chapter 12 contains this (“Each time Moses obeyed, he saw God act”). But the book’s main outline or thesis fails to capture the slow and steady progress a believer should attain through repeated experiences of intimacy that will lead him deeper into the knowledge of God. The spiral is a pedagogical gift, because it means learners can take a small, scared step now and discover God in it, rather than waiting until they “know Him enough.”

Part 2: Make the Course Learner-Centered and Activity-Based

2.1 Priority-ranked recommendations

  1. Turn the book into a curriculum: a chapter outcome, a 90-minute lesson template, and a one-page leader guide per session.
  2. Add a “Do” layer to every chapter: one practice, one partner, one testimony slot.
  3. Rebalance grace and surrender (Chapters 7, 8, 9, 11), and add the missing “Who is God?” and gospel content.
  4. Add Pentecostal-specific content: Spirit-filled boldness (Acts 4), prayer and worship as pathways, discernment criteria.
  5. Contextualize with real Indian scenarios and a plurality module.
  6. Fix the exegetical issues in the table above.
  7. Consolidate Part I from 11 chapters to about 7, and interleave stories with concepts.
  8. Rewrite the discussion questions (see below).
  9. Run a short leader-training session and set group safety norms.
  10. Proofread, and settle the title and the Bible version.

2.2 A standard 90-minute template for every session

Time (min) Phase Purpose
0–10 Gather: prayer, worship, check-in on last week’s practice Accountability; testimony
10–25 ACTIVITY: Open the Word, inductive study of a short passage Learners discover before being told
25–35 Teach: ≤10 minutes, 2–3 key points Content kept lean
35–60 ACTIVITY: role play, case study, or guided practice Active learning
60–72 Apply: one specific, dated action step Application
72–82 Check: teach-back or exit card Evidence of learning
82–90 Send: pray for one another; practice for the week Closure; transformation

2.3 Shifting from lecture to facilitation

2.3.1 Convert chapters into guided inductive study.

  • Ask
  • Observe (What does the text say?),
  • Interpret (What does it mean?),
  • Apply (What will I do?).
    • Replace “What stood out to you?” with questions that require the text: “What does verse 35 tell us Jesus did, when, and where?”
    • Use case studies drawn from learners' lives (see the note on contextextualisation below), short role plays (explaining “why only Jesus?” to a colleague), and testimony slots.

2.3.2 Reflection, application and accountability

Suggestions:

  • Weekly partner pairs (same gender, same stage of life) who exchange one-line WhatsApp check-ins on day 3 and day 6.
  • A one-page practice card for each chapter: what I'll do, when, where, what could stop me, how I'll recover.
  • A pocket journal prompt: “One thing I noticed about God this week.”
  • Start every session with 5 minutes of testimony from last week’s practice. This normalizes trying and failing.

2.3.3 Spiritual formation fitted to a Pentecostal setting

Suggestions:

  • Teach “waiting on the Lord” as a devotional practice, and invite learners to pray in whatever way is their normal practice.
  • Add a simple Scripture-Spirit-Community test for impressions and prophecies. That is, ask questions such as: Does it agree with Scripture? Does it point to Christ? Is it confirmed by mature believers? Does it produce love and obedience?
  • Include prayer/ intercession at the end of sessions (pairs pray for one another’s specific commitment).
  • Add worship, fasting and service as explicit pathways (alongside the five mentioned in Chapter 5).

2.3.3 Adapting to the Indian context

  • Case studies: a manager asks you to inflate figures; parents press for a marriage match that conflicts with your faith or values; a Hindu friend says “all paths lead to the same God”; job loss and EMIs; a colleague mocks your faith.
  • A plurality module (after Chapters 3 or 4): how to explain “knowing God through Jesus” respectfully and simply.
  • Secret place, realistically: commute, pre-dawn, office lunch break, earphones with an audio Bible, a rooftop or terrace; normalize 10 minutes.
  • Oral learners: tell stories aloud, use songs and short memory verses, allow prayer in mother tongue.
  • Honor/shame culture: confession-style questions (“What excuse are you making?”) should be private (written, then optionally shared) rather than public.
  • Name real persecution (family opposition, anti-conversion pressures) when discussing Paul and Daniel, and give leaders a referral path to the pastor.

2.3.4 Low-resource and mixed-ability options

  • Nothing requires printing: index cards or notebook paper are enough.
  • Mixed English fluency: allow answers in mother tongue, pair stronger readers with less confident ones, and use drawing or one-word responses.
  • Groups of 5 to 8 can do the lesson as one group; groups of 15 to 20 should split into groups of three or four.

2.3.5 Rewrite the discussion questions

Reduce to 3–4 per chapter in three types:
- Discover: a text-based question.
- Discuss: an open question with no “right” answer.
- Do: a specific action for this week.

Part 3: A Model Lesson (redesign of Chapter 5, “Pathways to Know God”)

Why Chapter 5. It is the hinge between information and practice: the only chapter that lists how. It is also the one most compressed (five pathways in four pages with no instructions), so it benefits most from redesign.

Title: Friends Grow Close on Purpose: Pathways to Know God
Big idea: Knowing God deepens like a friendship: through chosen, repeated habits, not by accident, and not by earning anything.
Audience: Young adult professionals and students; 5–20 members; lay-led; 90 minutes.

Learning outcomes
- Knowledge: Name the five pathways (Scripture, the Spirit, surrender, solitude and silence, community) and explain in their own words how each helps a friendship with God grow.
- Attitude: Express a desire to pursue God as a friend rather than perform for Him as a duty.
- Practice: Experience a 10-minute guided stillness with Scripture, then design one specific, realistic practice for the coming week and commit to it with a partner.

Preparation and materials
- Leader: read Chapter 5 and Mark 1:29–39. Do the 10-minute stillness practice yourself beforehand. Prepare a two-minute personal testimony about one pathway. Prepare the five pathways on a board or a sheet.
- Learners: Bible or phone Bible, paper or index cards, pen. A phone timer.

Timed lesson plan

Time Phase What happens Why it’s included
0–10 Opening and hook
(What's a hook? 🎞️)		 Pray and invite the Holy Spirit; a worship song. Each person thinks of someone they know really well and writes three words on how that closeness happened. Share in pairs (3 min). Leader links: knowing God grows the same way: time, conversation, shared experience. Starts from learners' lives; connects to the chapter’s friendship analogy; prayer sets the tone.
10–25 Scripture (inductive) In trios, read Mark 1:32–39. Observe: What happened the evening before? When and where did Jesus go? What did the disciples say, and what did He do? Interpret: why did He need this? Apply: what might this look like for me? Report back (5 min). Learners discover the practice in Jesus’s own life, rather than being told.
25–35 Core teaching (10 min, interactive) Leader presents the five pathways in two sentences each, with one question per pathway (“Where have you experienced this?”). Stress: these are ways a friendship grows, not a checklist to earn God’s favor. Learners silently rate each pathway 0–10 on a paper strip. Brief and interactive. Safeguards against works-righteousness.
35–60 Learner activities Practice (10 min): guided stillness. Settle for 2 minutes; read Psalm 46:10 slowly twice; sit in silence while “waiting on the Lord,” each in their normal way of prayer; jot what they noticed. Pairs (5 min): share one thing, or “nothing, and that’s okay.” Pathway audit (10 min): circle the strongest and the most neglected pathway. Choose one to strengthen. Experience before explanation: the “getting into the water” the book lacks. The “nothing happened” option protects honesty.
60–72 Application and commitment Complete the practice card: This week I will for minutes at (when), at (where). What could stop me: . My plan: . Pairs adjust it to be realistic (commute, shared room, office lunch). Agree on day-3 and day-6 WhatsApp check-ins. Specific, dated, and accountable. Small enough to succeed.
72–82 Check for understanding Teach-back (6 min): each person explains one pathway to a partner in 30 seconds, then switches. Exit card (4 min): (1) One pathway I can explain to a friend. (2) The step I'm committing to. (3) One question I still have. Leader collects the cards. Evidence of learning; informs next week.
82–90 Closing and take-home Pair prayer for each other’s specific commitment. Leader closes in prayer and reminds them of the practice card. Pentecostal prayer ministry; community support.

Facilitator notes
- Key questions to keep returning to: “What did you notice?” and “What could you realistically do this week?”
- Likely misunderstandings:
- “If I miss my practice, God is distant.” Reply: a friendship isn't broken by a missed day.
- Stillness is “emptying the mind” or yoga. Reply: Christian stillness fills the mind with Scripture and attends to God.
- “My impressions carry the authority of Scripture.” Remind: all impressions are tested by Scripture, Christ-centeredness and mature believers.
- Comparison with a more fluent peer: remind learners that this is a personal pace.
- Sensitive or contested points: people who “felt nothing”; those carrying grief, who may feel silence as absence; unavailable quiet space at home (affirm the commute, a stairwell). Never force sharing. Refer anyone showing signs of deeper struggle to the pastor.
- Contested practices: if someone raises tongues, silence or prophecy, answer briefly and refer back to Scripture, and don't let it become a debate.

Follow-up for the coming week
- Daily 10 minutes with the practice card; one line in a pocket journal (“What did I notice about God?”).
- Partner check-in messages on days 3 and 6.
- Next week opens with five minutes of testimony.

4.1 Terminal Course Objectives

By the end of this study, participants will be able to:

# Objective Primary domain
TCO1 Explain in their own words how God has revealed Himself, why Jesus Christ is the fullest revelation, and what it means to know God rather than know about Him. Cognitive
TCO2 Establish and sustain a personal rhythm of Scripture, prayer, stillness and worship for at least four consecutive weeks. Spiritual practice
TCO3 Evaluate spiritual impressions, teachings and experiences against Scripture, using a simple set of tests. Cognitive (higher order)
TCO4 Identify personal barriers to knowing God and take one concrete step (repentance, forgiveness, or reordering priorities). Affective and behavioral
TCO5 Apply the character of God to a real challenge in work, study or family by drafting and carrying out an action step of courageous obedience. Application
TCO6 Share their own story of knowing God, encourage another believer, and explain simply to a friend of another faith what it means to know God through Christ. Relational and missional

4.2 Domain-Specific Learning Objectives

Cognitive (knowing and understanding)

  • C1 Describe the ways God has revealed Himself and why Christ is the fullest revelation. (Understand)
  • C2 Contrast general and special revelation, knowing about versus knowing God, and biblical blessings versus prosperity expectations. (Analyze)
  • C3 Interpret a passage in its context using Observe–Interpret–Apply. (Apply, Analyze)
  • C4 Evaluate spiritual impressions and teachings against Scripture. (Evaluate)

Affective (attitudes and values)

  • A1 Express reverence for and desire for God Himself rather than only His benefits. (Value)
  • A2 Acknowledge personal barriers and need for grace without shame. (Respond)
  • A3 Choose to trust God’s character in uncertainty. (Commit)

Spiritual practice (experiential and behavioral)

  • B1 Maintain regular time in Scripture and prayer. (Apply)
  • B2 Practise stillness and waiting on the Spirit. (Apply)
  • B3 Practise obedience, confession and forgiveness. (Apply)
  • B4 Participate in worship and fasting as pathways. (Apply)

Relational (community)

  • R1 Maintain a weekly accountability partnership. (Apply)
  • R2 Give and receive testimony and encouragement. (Create)

Missional and vocational

  • M1 Make a courageous decision in work, study or family consistent with Scripture. (Apply)
  • M2 Explain “knowing God” in simple, respectful language to a person of another faith or none. (Create)

Assessment strategy

  • Baseline and endline: a 10-item “Know God Inventory” (1–5 scale), done in week 1 and again at the end.
  • Ongoing: practice cards, exit cards, and partner check-ins.
  • Culminating: a testimony evening where each learner shares one change, in a sentence or two.

Part 5: Your Next Steps

Phase 1: Decide the foundations

A. Reframe the course as a study of God, and say so.

Keep the best of the current thesis (Daniel 11:32, Philippians 3:10) as the goal. Make the content a systematic encounter with God's nature (who He is), attributes (what He is like), and ways (how He acts and relates to people). Settle one title, and write a one-paragraph course purpose that every lesson serves.

B. Write the Terminal Course Objectives first.

Write 5 or 6 measurable statements covering knowledge (know God's nature, attributes and ways), attitude (worship, trust, desire for God Himself), and practice (sustained spiritual practices, courageous obedience, sharing one's faith). Use verbs such as explain, identify, evaluate, practise, apply, share, never understand or appreciate. Refer to Part 4 of this review for a starting point.

C. Derive the unit and lesson structure from those objectives, not from the existing chapters.

Build backward: for each terminal objective, ask what learners must know, feel and do, and which lessons will get them there. One workable arrangement:

  • Unit 1: God makes Himself known (revelation: general and special, Scripture, Christ, the Spirit; knowing versus knowing about).
  • Unit 2: Who God is (the Trinity and Father-heart; holiness, love, justice, mercy, faithfulness, sovereignty, power, wisdom, presence; each as its own lesson).
  • Unit 3: How God works with people (covenant, providence, discipline, suffering, His voice and guidance, answered and unanswered prayer).
  • Unit 4: Responding to God (barriers, surrender and its cost, the practices, community, boldness and mission).
  • Unit 5: Lives that knew God (the biographical studies, now placed after learners have the concepts, as case studies of what they've learned).

Avoid over-lapping of content across lessons

D. Write a specific objective for every lesson

It must be in three domains (know / feel-value / do), and fill in an alignment matrix:

lesson objective → Scripture → learner activity → evidence of learning.

No lesson goes into development until its row is complete.

Phase 2: Build the content

A. Assemble the Scripture map before writing any lesson.

For each lesson, list the passages learners will study inductively.

Include the omitted texts: Jeremiah 9:23-24; Exodus 34:6-7; Job 42:5; Matthew 7:21-23; John 10:14-15, Galatians 4:9 and 1 Corinthians 8:3 (being known by God); Jeremiah 22:15-16; Ephesians 3:14-21; 1 Corinthians 1:18-31; John 1:18 and 2 Corinthians 4:6; Acts 17:22-31; Psalm 9:10; 1 Samuel 2:12 and 3:7; Deuteronomy 6:4-5; Isaiah 40 and 43:10; Hosea 2:19-20; 1 John 4:7-8; Romans 8:14-16; Matthew 11:25-30; and Ezekiel’s “you will know that I am the LORD” refrain.

Replace the heavily repeated verses (John 17:3, John 5:39, Philippians 3:10, John 14:21, Hosea 6:3) with these, quoting each repeated verse in one lesson only.

Choose one Bible translation, state it, and check every quotation against it.

B. Make "Who is God?" the center of the course.

Each attribute lesson should be anchored in a primary text where God describes Himself (Exodus 34:6-7 for mercy and faithfulness; Isaiah 6 for holiness; Jeremiah 9:23-24 for love, justice and righteousness; Isaiah 40 for incomparability and power; Romans 8 for the Father's heart), shown in Jesus (as Chapter 3 does well), and applied to a real situation.

C. Build in the gospel and grace from the start.

Add a lesson, early in the course, on how a person enters and is assured in relationship with God (grace, repentance, faith, assurance), so the course has a door for seekers and nominal members. Make being known by God first the frame for every later call to the pursuit of God, and reframe the surrender and cost material (old Ch 9) as a response to mercy, anchored in Romans 12:1 and Galatians 2:20—not a condition for relationship.

D. Add the Pentecostal content that is missing.

Include Acts 4 and Spirit-filled boldness; Romans 8 and "Abba" as the Spirit's work in *relational* knowing; prayer, worship and fasting as full pathways; and a short, practical set of discernment tests for impressions and prophecies (agrees with Scripture, exalts Christ, confirmed by mature believers, produces love and obedience). State Pentecostal distinctives plainly, and do not let them go unmentioned.

E. Correct the exegetical and doctrinal issues

Use the table in Part 1. Do this while writing the lessons, not afterwards. Start with the old Ch 9 (grace-balance), 11 (the two-groups divide), 12 (the Moses "fearless" claim), 14 (arguments from silence) and 15 (the Judges refrain and the women-in-leadership question). Source the "four ways of knowing" typology or drop it. Mark inference as inference. Add a gracious, short note on the evangelical view of Mary, for the sake of Catholic participants.

Phase 3: Design each lesson

A. Adopt the model lesson as the template for every lesson.

If you are in agreement with the 90-minute lesson structure (gather and check-in; inductive Scripture study; ten-minute teaching; learner activity; application card; check for understanding; closing prayer and send), let it be fixed. Else, modify it suitably. Each lesson fills in its own objectives, passages, activity, and facilitator notes. Add to the template: a “Why each element is included” note, a “likely-misunderstandings” box, and a “this week's practice” card.

B. Replace the chapter discussion questions

Include three types of questions per lesson:

  • Discover (a question that sends learners into the text),
  • Discuss (open, no single right answer),
  • Do (a specific, dated action).

Keep it to 3 or 4 questions. Put confession-style questions in writing, privately, before any optional sharing.

C. Design for active, evidence-bearing learning in every lesson.

Include inductive study, case studies, role plays, guided practice, teach-back, and an exit card. Weekly partner pairs, mid-week check-ins and opening testimony form the accountability thread. Do not allow a lesson that is mostly exposition.

Phase 4: Fit it to the learners and the leaders

D. Contextualize for urban Indian young adults.

Write 3 or 4 case studies from their actual lives (workplace integrity, marriage and family pressure, job insecurity, a colleague's mockery) and use them across lessons. Add a short lesson or module on knowing God through Christ among many religions, drawing on Acts 17, with practice in respectful conversation. Name real family and social opposition, and take cultural realities (shared rooms, commutes, oral learners, mother-tongue prayer, honor and shame) into account in the practices.

E. Write a leader guide for each lesson and a short training.

Each lesson gets a one-page guide: timings, key questions, answers to expect, misunderstandings, and what to do if the discussion stalls. Hold a short training where leaders *experience* the first lesson before leading it. Include group safety norms (confidentiality, no forced sharing, no advice-giving), guidance for people who felt nothing, and a clear referral path to the pastor for grief, abuse, or serious struggle.

F. Build assessment into the design.

Use a short baseline and endline inventory, exit cards, practice cards and partner check-ins, and a culminating testimony session. Each lesson's evidence of learning must match its stated objective.

Phase 5: Test and finish

G. Pilot before publishing.

Run Lessons 1 to 3 with two or three care cells, including at least one led by a lay leader who has not seen the material. Collect: Did learners reach the objectives? Did the timing work? Which activities fell flat? Where did leaders struggle? Revise, then roll out.

H. Produce a clean, consistent final version.

Proofread (typographical errors, the duplicated table of contents, broken link artifacts), keep one title, one translation and one set of terms throughout, and produce separate Participant and Leader editions.

Part 6: A Note On Production

If you use MS-Word …

MS-Word is not the best application to create any book. However, if you choose to create your book using a Word Processor, these are my observations and suggestions to those responsible for the production of the training material.

  • Any document created in MS Word or similar word processing applications must use the built-in "Styles" feature in order to give the document proper structure. There are built-in styles such as Heading-1, Heading-2, Heading-3, Body, Body Text, etc. that can be customised. Every paragraph in the document must be aligned to one of the built-in styles. This helps content creators to think clearly and structure their document properly.
  • One of the benefits of using built-in styles is that it enables us to automatically create a Table of Contents. This document did not have a table of contents.
  • Another benefit of using built-in styles: it saves time. Those who type in the content need not rely on time-consuming and arbitrary formatting of each line or paragraph because they will use pre-formatted styles.

Consider Using An Online Learning Platform

Once the draft of your training material is ready, you might wish to create lessons (and future courses) on a free and open-source learning platform such as Moodle. The learning-curve is slightly steep but the rewards are substantial. Extensive online documentation is available to help you set it up on your church website or on Moodle's own site. A number of churches, Sunday Schools, and schools use Moodle.
