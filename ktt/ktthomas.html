Preface

The life stories of godly saints have always been a source of inspiration and motivation. Church history backs that up. The poet Longfellow once said that reading about great men and women reminds us of what our own lives could become. Dr. Freeman put it another way: when great people make history, the rest of us are just spectators. But when you read a biography, you actually become part of the story. That says a lot about why biography matters.

I have known Pastor K. T. Thomas for over two decades, and writing his life story is something I feel genuinely privileged to do. I’ll be honest, though. I almost said no. Biography is a serious undertaking. The writer has to hold himself to high standards, navigate real complications, and work within the limits of their own knowledge and experience. I also wrestled with a specific question: should a biographer simply present a person’s character, or should he offer honest analysis and critique as well? Pastor Thomas himself settled that for me. He told me wholeheartedly that he welcomed critical evaluation. With that assurance, I felt free to move forward—to analyze, explain, critique, and assess, so that you, the reader, can draw your own informed conclusions.

Most of what you will find in these pages comes from things I personally heard and learned directly from Pastor Thomas. In many ways, his biography is also a history of the Indian Pentecostal Church Northern Region (IPCNR). What began as an incredibly small, modest work grew into something remarkable; and behind that growth lies a story of hardship, tears, and sacrifice that only he fully knows. May the Lord honor His servant appropriately on the day He distributes rewards.

When I look at the great things God has accomplished through this man, I sometimes wonder whether he has received the recognition he deserves. Perhaps the reason is this: he never softened his rebukes to spare anyone’s feelings, and his uncompromising evaluations did not always make him popular.

Many people helped me put this manuscript together under significant time pressure. I am deeply grateful to Pastor K. T. Thomas and his family, Evangelist P. T. Alexander, the IPC Northern Region office staff, and the Bible school students for all they contributed. I also want to thank Dr. Oommachen, Associate Professor at Jabalpur University, who carefully reviewed and revised the [Malayalam] manuscript despite his own demanding schedule; and my wife Rosamma, who wrote out the manuscript by hand so beautifully. I offer this work at the feet of the Almighty, praying that He will use it to bless many.

Your brother in the service of the King of kings,

Publisher’s Note

The India Pentecostal Church of God, Northern Region, established in 1969, carries a history that is, from first to last, a story of miraculous growth, extraordinary sacrifice, and the boundless grace of God. The story of Pastor K. T. Thomas, the servant of the Lord whom God raised up to helm that growth, is in a very real sense the history of the IPCNR itself.

In the vast and largely unyielding mission fields of northern India, this beloved servant of the Lord surrendered his life entirely to the gospel, laboring without rest, day and night. The hardships he endured were no small matter. The chronicle of each passing day in his life is, at its core, a chronicle of divine strength sustaining a man through suffering. He resigned from a secure government position and stepped into full-time gospel ministry—not impulsively, but in response to a clear divine call and a definite God-given vision. What began as a modest, almost fragile work has grown into an expansive movement, forged through trials of every kind. Across all the states of northern India, his ministry now stretches wide. As he shared with me, during the writing of this book, many experiences from those thirty-plus years of service that he had never spoken of to anyone, I found myself deeply stirred.

Most movements operating in northern India depend entirely on foreign missions or individual foreign donors for their sustenance. The IPCNR, by contrast, is funded primarily through the sincere and sacrificial support of Malayali brothers and sisters. Beyond its work across the various states of India, the IPCNR also carries on ministry in Nepal, Bhutan, and Sikkim. Since Christian activity is prohibited by law in those regions, evangelism is done in a discrete manner. Believers who wish to be baptized are often forced to cross the border into India. It is a field fraught with danger. Yet the Lord has continued, in ways both wonderful and astonishing, to see them through.

It is my personal conviction that it would be difficult to find another individual in whom so many qualities essential to the Lord’s work are so thoroughly and harmoniously combined: sincerity, integrity, a spirit of sacrifice, faithfulness to duty, a sense of justice, and fervent zeal. It is true that the rigor that springs from his zeal, and his unrelenting stand for truth and righteousness without yielding so much as a fraction, have earned him no small number of adversaries. Yet anyone who has worked closely alongside him will readily discern that behind what may at times appear to be a stern exterior lies a heart brimming with brotherly love, tender care, compassion, and the grace of God. I have personally witnessed him lose sleep when support owed to a fellow worker was delayed even slightly. Such is the depth of his concern for his co-laborers in the ministry.

Few servants of God can have endured persecution and contempt to the degree he has, and without just cause. Yet he has received divine grace in abundance to face it all with courage and without faltering. Had his elevated Christian vision, his extraordinary spiritual fortitude, and his unwavering sense of purpose been even a little less than they are, he would long since have worn out and retreated. That he is alive today is itself a living demonstration of the power of God confounding the conclusions of medical science.

He has made it a particular priority to encourage young men and women, training them, creating opportunities for their gifts to develop, and lifting them by the hand into positions of leadership. This is a quality rarely seen among Malayali Pentecostal church leaders, and it sets him apart. I state all of this not because I am his son-in-law, but because these are facts I have personally witnessed, heard, and experienced in the course of working alongside him in the gospel field.

What the Lord said of John the Baptist – that he was a burning and shining lamp – is equally true of this servant of the Lord. In the gospel fields of northern India, he is indeed a lamp that burns and shines. That stream of light has guided many into the true light. Yet the burning that lies behind the shining, the consuming process that makes the light possible, goes largely unseen. And he bears no grievance over that. Like many others who have had the privilege of working with him, I too have come to understand clearly that he carries out his ministry in full surrender to the hands of the Lord, receiving divine counsel as he goes.

This biography came to be written primarily through the initiative of Pastor K. Y. Geevarghese, the current minister of Green Park Church and President of Pentecostal Young People’s Association (PYPA) Northern Region. He labored day and night with the goal of completing the manuscript within a mere three months.

On this occasion, we wish to express our sincere gratitude to Dr. Oommachen for contributing a fitting foreword to this volume, and to Pastor P. L. Paramjyothi, Pastor John Daniel, Dr. Kurien Thomas, Dr. Thomas Mathew, Dr. P. P. Job, Pastor K. V. Abraham, and Evangelist Sunny George for their warm messages of blessing. We are also grateful to the team at Sabina Printing Press for the excellence of their work in printing this volume. Special thanks are due to Theophine John for the Malayalam computer photo-composing, and to Joy Thomas for the design, proofreading, and related work. That PYPA Northern Region was able to play a part in this publication is, without question, a matter of lasting pride for the organization. We have no doubt that this book will prove to be a source of spiritual enrichment for many. We pray that through it, many young people would be moved to step forward as bold witnesses of the gospel into northern India, where hundreds of millions have yet to hear the name of Christ. I join you in praying for the continued health and long life of our dear servant of the Lord. May the Lord continue to use him abundantly in the gospel fields of northern India—and may He grant each one of us the grace to experience his ministry yet more fully.

Foreword:
Pastor K. T. Thomas, A Man of God

In His sovereign grace and boundless mercy, God condescends to raise up certain individuals from obscure backgrounds and unlikely circumstances, granting them the power and grace to live their lives for His glory. In this way He has called forth heroes of faith across many lands and many ages: men such as Martin Luther, John Knox, William Carey, Hudson Taylor, David Livingstone, and R. F. Cooke. In our own land of India, God likewise raised up eminent servants in Pastors K. E. Abraham, K. C. Cherian, P. M. Samuel, T. M. Varughese, A. R. T. Athisayam, A. C. Samuel, M. Benjamin, M. K. Chacko, T. G. Oommen, P. T. Chacko, and many others who pioneered the Pentecostal movement and finished their race of faith at the Master’s call to come home. We are grateful to the Lord for the faithful servants who remain among us. Among them Pastors Paramjyothi, Kurien Thomas, K. T. Thomas, P. K. Chacko, P. M. Philip, P. D. Johnson, P. A. V. Sam, and Evangelist P. I. Abraham — who continue to carry the flame forward in obedience to the great commission of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

The life and witness of Pastor K. T. Thomas, the subject of this biography, stands as a remarkable demonstration of God’s sovereign grace and tender mercy. The unmistakable presence and power of the Lord that so many have sensed upon coming into personal contact with this man of God bears full testimony to the transforming power of Jesus Christ. Pastor Thomas has always worked with clear vision and conviction, expecting great things from God and attempting great things for God, and therefore accomplishing great things, steering the lives of hundreds of men and women toward God and toward the extension of His kingdom. The pages of this biography bear witness to that story.

In John 16:33, our Lord declares: “In the world you will have tribulation; but be of good cheer, I have overcome the world.” These words carry prophetic weight, not only for the twelve disciples, but for all who genuinely deny themselves, take up their cross, and follow Him. At the call of Christ, Pastor Thomas resigned his position with the Government of India and chose instead a path marked by hardship and trial, which he has faced with the discipline, courage, and cheerfulness of a seasoned soldier of God, winning many battles for the King of kings. God has honored this humble man by using him for the salvation of thousands, the spiritual growth of many believers, the training and equipping of workers for Christian ministry, and the planting of churches across the hard soil of northern India. A significant number of the churches currently within the IPC Northern Region exist today as the direct fruit of his labors and sacrificial service.

It was through the late Pastor M. K. Chacko, whose biography I wrote in 1978, that I first learned of Pastor K. T. Thomas and the ministry he was carrying on in Delhi and other parts of India. In the years that followed, I had many opportunities to come to know him personally and to hear his remarkable testimonies, which never fail to stir and inspire. Having now read carefully through the manuscript of this biography compiled by Pastor K. Y. Geevarghese, I count it a privilege to offer these few words concerning this great servant of God. This abridged account of a surrendered life is brought before the reader with the single aim and earnest expectation that the Lord will graciously use it as a source of inspiration and encouragement, drawing its readers into the same burden and compassion that Pastor K. T. Thomas carries for perishing souls. Let us pray earnestly that God will grant Pastor Thomas many more fruitful years of ministry, that he may continue his vital leadership among God’s people. May the Almighty make this book a channel of blessing to all who read it.

A Vessel for Honor

I am pleased to write a few words of appreciation for this biography of Pastor K. T. Thomas, my longtime friend, senior colleague, and fellow laborer in North India.

I first met him in 1963 during the Delhi Easter Convention conducted by the late Pastor M. K. Chacko. I had just arrived in North India and was on my way to Rajasthan. Those were the early days of Pastor Thomas’s ministry. The remarkable zeal and energy of this young servant of God immediately caught my attention. Since that time, I have had the privilege of knowing him personally. His wife, Sister Marykutty, who has faithfully shared in his labors and sacrifices, also comes from my hometown of Poonaloor.

Pastor Thomas’ growth as a pioneer church planter and spiritual leader has been truly remarkable. God’s hand has clearly been upon his life, and he has proven to be a vessel “fit for the Master’s use.” Like many servants of God, he has experienced his share of disappointments and hardships. Yet what appeared to be setbacks often became opportunities in the hands of God. The sustaining grace of the Chief Shepherd enabled him to persevere through every trial.

Scripture declares, “My righteous servant shall live by faith.” Pastor Thomas left a secure and promising career to follow God’s call in faith. He, his wife, and their children endured many hardships—hardships that can only be fully understood by those who have served the Lord by faith in North India. Through it all, God remained faithful. He is never indebted to those who serve Him. As the Psalmist writes, “Let the Lord be magnified, who delights in the prosperity of His servant” (Psalm 35:27). Sadly, not everyone shares that same delight when God blesses His servants.

I am reminded of these lines from H. W. Longfellow’s A Psalm of Life:

“The heights by great men reached and kept
Were not achieved by sudden flight
But they, while their companions slept
Were toiling upward in the night.

Lives of great men all remind us
We can make our lives sublime
And, departing, leave behind us
Footprints in the sands of time.”

Pastor Thomas has already left many footprints in the sands of time, and I believe there are still more to come. There is a saying that “the wind and the waves are always on the side of the ablest navigator.” In many ways, that saying reflects the challenging journey of Pastor Thomas’s life and ministry. Through storms, opposition, and adversity, he has continued to move forward by faith and determination.

It is my prayer that the testimony recorded in this biography will inspire many young men and women to step out in faith and serve the Lord in North India. May God use this account of His faithfulness to encourage a new generation to answer His call and devote themselves wholeheartedly to His work.

Udaipur
Feb 15, 1989 		Dr. Thomas Mathews Ph.D

Foreword

It was at a crossroads in my life, when I was yet a sinner, steeped in wrongdoing, that the holy God found me. By His love-filled, sacred call, He drew this poor servant unto Himself, granted me a vision of the Cross, and brought deep conviction of the terrifying nature of sin and its inevitable consequence: divine judgment. He led me into profound repentance, blotted out my many transgressions, and cleansed me by His sacred and precious blood. He flooded my darkness-filled life with light, poured His eternal love into my innermost being, and strengthened me with divine power; power to declare with full assurance: this God is my God. With a heart overflowing in gratitude and eyes brimming with tears, this poor servant recalls the eternal purposes, the boundless mercies, the immeasurable love, and the unspeakable providences of this most gracious and great God. For all that this poor servant has experienced in life until this hour, and for all that is yet to come, all glory is continually and humbly offered to the Almighty God.

Wherever this poor servant has gone, across India and beyond, carrying the gospel commission of the Lord Christ who saved me, I have made it a practice to open with a personal testimony of what God has done in my life. Many friends, brothers and sisters in the faith, and fellow servants of the Lord have repeatedly urged me to commit these blessings and experiences, received from the Lord throughout my life, to the form of a book. Yet whenever I reflect on the grace and love this most merciful God has shown me, and on the wondrous works and remarkable goodness He has wrought in the life of this poor servant’s family, I am seized by a recurring fear: would writing about things I was entirely unworthy to receive amount to nothing more than self-congratulation? Beyond that, I am troubled by the thought that fallen humanity, so often incapable of seeing as God sees or looking as God looks, prone instead to suspicion and prejudice, looking for and finding and judging not the good in a person but only the evil, might read such an account and conclude that the testimony of this poor servant is further diminished rather than strengthened. Above all, I am apprehensive because even within today’s Pentecostal world, the greater part no longer believes in divine visions or in the miraculous works of God; and even those who once believed have largely lost that ground, drifting into worldliness, materialism, self-interest, and the love of money, so that such an account might only give occasion for some to dismiss it as hollow exaggeration and affix upon it a mark of contempt.

When the Indian Pentecostal Church Northern Region (IPCNR) was established in 1969, it consisted of no more than fourteen congregations and a few outstation ministries. By the grace of God, the Lord in His mercy has caused that number to grow to approximately one hundred and fifty. In those early days, the work was launched in the following manner: from the five hundred rupees paid monthly in advance by the Green Park congregation as rent for our Faith Home (parsonage), this poor servant would set aside fifty, seventy-five, or at most one hundred rupees each month to encourage a few needy co-workers in the ministry. Subsequently, the needs were met through the meager tithes and offerings received from congregations and ministers, along with occasional personal contributions sent by brothers and sisters who had gone abroad. It is therefore a matter of profound wonder that a work which began with barely five thousand rupees annually has, by the grace of God, grown to the point where over six hundred thousand rupees are now expended each year. It was the deep personal conviction that God had specifically entrusted this responsibility to this poor servant that made it possible, by grace, to labor with such sacrifice. It is worth mentioning in this connection that the original vision of this poor servant – to support at least ten gospel workers in the Lord’s service using funds given by Indians – is not worth mentioning considering what has now come to pass.

My dear brother and the author of this volume, Pastor Geevarghese, has long urged this poor servant to put the story of his experiences into writing, and ultimately pressed forward by offering to undertake the writing himself. Many of my diaries have been lost, and a severe stroke suffered in February 1985 has caused numerous events to recede into the shadows of forgetfulness. What I could recall, I shared with him personally; and he gathered further information through such diaries as remained and through various records of the IPCNR. He is, moreover, a person who knows us well: following his baptism, he came to the congregation at V-10, Green Park, Delhi, where this poor servant had been ministering since 1968, shared his testimony, worshipped with us, and from that time forward maintained a close relationship with this poor servant, with our family, and with our ministry. Beyond all this, he served as an IPCNR evangelist from 1970, received ordination and was appointed as a minister in 1974, served for several years in Delhi itself, and faithfully engaged in the general ministries of the IPCNR in various capacities. That such a trustworthy servant of the Lord took it upon himself with enthusiasm and effort to write this account is something for which I am deeply grateful. When he read aloud to me what he had written, I offered praise to God. The exact timing of certain events is not always certain to me, and it is possible that some matters have not been recorded in strict chronological order. Many of the blessings and experiences this poor servant has received from God in family life have not been included here. As I read through these pages, many things seem even now as though they belong to a dream. I am weak, inadequate, and unworthy.

Many of the qualities that ought to be found in servants of God are absent in me. More than that, if others knew me as I know myself, the world would surely pronounce its verdict that I am worthy of none of this. Yet, remembering God’s grace, His compassion, and His great patience, I praise Him.

Yahweh is compassionate and gracious… His righteousness extends to their children’s children. (Psalm 103:8–17)

May this worthy endeavor of my dear brother prove to be a blessing to all its readers. May it please God that through the reading of this account, at least some souls would find salvation; and that others, placing their trust in the Almighty God who has led this poor servant and his family in triumph along the path of faith until this present hour, would follow Him steadfastly and without wavering. Praying that they too may always lean upon God alone, and that His gracious favor would rest upon them, this poor servant humbly presents this small volume at the footstool of the Almighty.

Your brother and fellow laborer in Christ,

F-58, Green Park,
New Delhi 100 016

The Life Divine

For pleasures brief and passing sweet
I spurn’d the counsel of my God;
The serpent’s wound brought death within,
My soul sank ’neath His chast’ning rod;
My days were veil’d in deepest night,
And grief attended all my trod.

Yet He whose love knows neither bound
Nor change beheld my ruin still,
And by the light of truth revealed
Did bend mine eyes and heart and will;
His radiance flooded all my soul,
And grace poured life against the ill.

What am I, frail and poor, that He
Should rain such treasures from above—
Eternal life, eternal rest,
And endless fellowship of love?
Therefore with praise and thankful song
I bow before my Lord thereof.

Birth and Early Years

Family, Roots, and School Days

Kochoonni1 – who would later be known to the world as K. T. Thomas – was born on June 18, 1927, the firstborn son of Mr. Idichandy (Thomas) of the Nellilathey family2 and Dinamma of the Thondiyathu family of Kallissery.3 As the eldest child, he grew up surrounded by more than his share of affection and indulgence.

From early on, one trait set him apart from other boys his age: he had little interest in looking out only for himself or going it alone. Those who knew him well could see it clearly then, and those who know him now would say nothing has changed. As a young man, he ran with his friends and threw himself into the world with everything he had; as a man of God, that same energy now pours into spiritual pursuits alongside his brothers and sisters in the faith. Some things, it seems, are just wired in.

During his school years, he kept a wide circle of friends, and together they chased after the usual pleasures of youth without much thought for anything deeper. He did attend a Brethren Assembly4 Sunday school for a stretch in his younger years, though he has admitted with a smile that games and mischief consistently won out over Scripture memory.

His parents were committed members of the Brethren Assembly, and his father made a point of sending him to the Sunday morning Bible readings. More often than not, however, young K. T. and whatever friend had been sent along with him would quietly slip away to play instead.

His mother, Dinamma, was the daughter of Nainachan5 upadesi,6 himself an uncle of the celebrated poet K. V. Simon. She followed the teachings of the Brethren Assembly with deep reverence and devotion. K. T. Thomas has spoken more than once about the power of her prayer life, how she would rise in the middle of the night and call out his name before God. He testifies that her prayers, offered in the quiet dark of those early hours, have followed him and shaped him throughout his entire life. What a faithful God, who does not disappoint a mother who prays.

Leaving Home for the Military

After completing his matriculation, K. T. Thomas did something that would have been almost unthinkable by the standards of his upbringing: without his father’s knowledge or permission, and without so much as a word of farewell, he enlisted in the military and left home. His beloved father, a man he deeply loved, was not consulted. Not obeyed. Simply left behind.

Military life taught him the discipline of obedience in ways his school years never had. But God remained a stranger to him, and the opportunities to seek Him went ignored. Meanwhile, his parents were on their knees, praying through tears for the son who had gone, trusting that those prayers would find their way to heaven.

Three Brushes with Death

The year 1947 brought three moments when K. T. Thomas looked death in the face. And he has said that even now, decades later, the memory of each one sends a chill through him.

In the first, he was on duty late one night when a soldier with Pakistani sympathies appeared, pointing a loaded weapon at him and demanding access to certain arms. Thomas refused. The man lunged forward with a death threat. How God protected his life in that charged and terrifying moment is something he still speaks of as nothing short of a miracle.

The second came in Ferozpur, Punjab, where a massive dam gave way without warning and a wall of floodwater came roaring toward the town. Thomas and his fellow soldiers were on emergency duty trying to protect the area when the water suddenly encircled them. There was no way out; and then, out of nowhere, a military truck appeared, swept them up, and carried them to safety. A miracle. There is no other word for it.

That same night brought the third. Back at their quarters, the floodwaters rose again while they were asleep. People fled in every direction; some climbed trees, others scrambled onto rooftops. Buildings around them buckled and collapsed under the force of the current. Thomas and his companions took refuge on the upper floor of one such building. And it was one of the few that held. God kept it standing.

Looking back on those years, years when he was living as far from God as a man can live, Thomas is struck by the relentless mercy that was quietly tracking him the whole time. He had not sought God. He did not deserve God’s protection. And yet there it was, again and again. The depth of that love, he says, is simply beyond words.

A Life Coming Apart

As the years passed, K. T. Thomas drifted further and further from any spiritual moorings. It would be fair to say that faith had all but disappeared from his life entirely. He fell in with friends who had Communist leanings and began studying the ideology in earnest. Most of what he earned he spent freely — on pleasures, on friends, on whatever the world had to offer in that moment. His life was becoming what he himself later described as a boat adrift on the open sea, with no compass and no destination.

Things got bad enough that even his worldly friends – people with no particular moral standards of their own – began to shake their heads at him. At one point, a Hindu friend who was home on leave wrote to a mutual Muslim friend asking how K. T. Thomas was getting on. The reply he received said it plainly: “Thomas is just like a cigar burning from both ends.”

That was where things stood. He was burning through his life from both sides at once—fast, reckless, and headed toward nothing.

Marriage and Family Life

In April 1952, Thomas came home on leave. His parents seized the moment; they had a marriage in mind, and they moved quickly. He pushed back at first, but eventually came around. Then, with just one week left before the wedding, he quietly made up his mind to slip away and head back to his post in Pune without telling anyone. God had other plans, he now says—and he says it with a grin.

On May 8, 1952, K. T. Thomas got married. His bride was Marykutty, a young woman of only sixteen and a committed member of the Brethren Assembly. She was the eldest daughter of Koshi, himself the eldest son of Geevarughese Koshi (an agent) of the Cherookara family branch in Ayroor, who had been living at Villamkonam Bungalow, Thrikkannamangal, Kottaarakkara. By the time of the wedding, Koshi had relocated to Poonaloor.1 It was through the ministry of Chacko upadesi of Poonaloor that this family, formerly members of the Jacobite tradition, had come to embrace the Brethren Assembly. The wedding was held at the Brethren Assembly Hall in Umayattookara, Kallissery, and was officiated by Thomas’s own uncle, Avrachen upadesi of the Cheroovalethu family in Kallissery. Thomas has said many times, with unmistakable pride, that Marykutty was exactly the partner God had prepared for him.

The Loss of His Father

Not long after the wedding, something painful happened that would mark Thomas deeply. His beloved father, the man he had defied, left behind, and never quite reconciled with, passed away. It had been only six months since the wedding. Thomas was still posted in Pune.

A few days before it happened, God, in His compassion, even toward a man who had turned his back on Him, showed Thomas through a dream what was coming. He woke up shaken and heavy-hearted. His friends tried to reassure him: “Dreams don’t come true like that,” they said. Then the telegram arrived: “Father critically ill.” He packed his things and set out for home immediately.

On the second evening of the journey, while standing at Egmore Railway Station in Madras, something came over him without warning. He sank to the ground and cried out, “Appaccha! Appaccha!” – Father! Father! – overcome with a grief he couldn’t explain. When he finally reached home, he understood: his father had passed away at that very hour.

Before leaving Pune, he had sent a telegram home: “On my way; wait for me.” He sent another from Madras. But by the time he arrived, the funeral had already been held the previous evening. There were no refrigeration facilities in those days to preserve the body, and with the telegrams delayed, the family had had no choice but to proceed without him.

He arrived in Kallissery the next morning. Before he even reached the house, word of his father’s death found him on the road. He stopped at his uncle’s house nearby, grief-stricken, eyes full of tears. Morning prayers had just ended, and those gathered came out to meet him. His uncle embraced him and wept, saying softly, “Son, this was God’s will.”

Something broke loose in Thomas. “Don’t you dare bring God’s name into my house,” he burst out. “Your God, the one who took my righteous father before his time is …” He went on, unparliamentary words pouring out in anguish. Everyone around him wept.

Carrying the Weight of a Family

Everyone who knew Thomas assumed that losing his father would jolt him into a better life. Instead, things got worse. As the eldest son, the full weight of the family now fell on his shoulders—his mother, his siblings, everything. It crushed him, and rather than steadying him, the pressure sent him further off the rails. Back in Pune, his drinking intensified. He was coming apart in broad daylight.

And yet, even through all of it, he kept sending money home. His mother, his sister, his two brothers, his wife — all of them depended on him. Everyone except his mother was still in high school. He was holding up an entire household while his own life was caving in.

Transfer to Secretariat in Delhi

Life moved on. In 1953, he received a transfer to Delhi. In 1955, he went back home, brought his wife to the capital, and for the first time they began building a life together under the same roof. Marykutty prayed constantly for her husband’s transformation, quietly, persistently, without giving up.

Around this time, a fellow in the army named Varghese, an officer and a member of the Brethren Assembly, made repeated attempts to share the gospel with Thomas, and prayed earnestly for his conversion. Another soldier, K. D. Timothy, a Pentecostal believer (now Pastor K. D. Timothy), also tried his best to get through to him on spiritual matters. Thomas’s response to all of it was consistent: he saw it coming and dodged it, every time, on purpose.

Eventually, a civilian posting opened up at the Central Secretariat in Delhi, and Thomas took it. He worked there for about a year before being assigned on deputation to the VIP Guest House.

A Son Is Born

On March 14, 1956, the Lord gave Thomas and Marykutty a son, a gift that brought a quiet joy into their home. They named him Rejimon (Stanley Thomas). His mother, a woman of deep faith, raised him with tender, devoted care.

Sister and Brother-in-Law Come to Delhi

Around this time, Thomas’s only sister, Mariamma (known as Podiyamma), got married. Thomas brought her and her new husband, Samuel John, to Delhi to live with them. Samuel John would go on to settle in Chandigarh, where he has served as General Minister for the Church of God movement in that region.

Conversion

After his son was born, something began to shift inside Thomas—quietly, almost imperceptibly at first. The faithful prayers of those who loved him were beginning to find their mark. For about four months, he carried a growing inner restlessness he couldn’t name or shake. There were moments when something close to anguish would sweep over him, and he didn’t know why. He turned it over in his mind repeatedly, troubled by a heaviness he couldn’t account for.

He would later say that one of the main reasons he had run so hard from God was that so many people who called themselves God’s children showed so little of God’s love in how they actually lived. That hypocrisy had been enough to keep him away for years. And yet, as those who knew him well have heard him say many times since: God had seen him in His foreknowledge all along. That thought alone would move him to praise.

A Prayer Meeting

Around this time, an unusual opportunity came his way. Pastor M. K. Chacko organized a special series of meetings in Janakpuri, Delhi, where Thomas was then living, bringing in the late Pastor K. V. Kuriachan from Kuttapuzha, Kerala, as the speaker. Thomas’s brother-in-law Samuel John, Timothy, and a Mar Thomite1 named T. J. Abraham (now Rev. T. J. Abraham) pressed him earnestly to go with his family, and he agreed. The message that evening reached him; he felt it land on his hardened heart; but he did not yield. Not yet.

Then came a particular evening when he came home from the office more unsettled than usual. Marykutty came to him quietly, asked what he needed, and went to the kitchen to brew tea. Left alone in the room, Thomas sat there looking around with a weight on him he could barely stand. His eyes fell on Marykutty’s Bible sitting on the table.

Something pulled him toward it. He picked it up reluctantly, since he had no real interest in the Bible. In fact, a few years earlier, when his mother had given him a Bible with the words, “Son, you must read this,” he had handed it off to a relative just to be rid of it. Now, almost against his will, he opened it. The first verse his eyes landed on was Ephesians 2:1. “And you He made alive, who were dead in trespasses and sins.”

His eyes filled with tears instantly. He got up, bolted the door so Marykutty wouldn’t see him, and sat down and wept. The thought of his wife finding him like this – a hard man, crying – felt like too much to bear. But the verse wouldn’t leave him alone. Am I dead in trespasses and sins? Is there actually a God? Is God dealing with me right now? The questions pressed in on him from all sides. After a while he wiped his face, stood up, and opened the door. Marykutty was just coming in with the tea. She could see that something had changed in him, but she didn’t say a word.

Saturday Prayer Meeting

The next day was Saturday. Near where they lived, a group of Christians from various denominations gathered every Saturday evening for prayer. After what had happened the night before, Thomas went. When he knocked quietly at the door, they looked at him with some suspicion – Why is he bothering us? – but he told them simply, “I’ve come to pray with you.” They weren’t sure whether to believe him, but they let him in, and he stayed for the whole meeting. Around this time, his sister and brother-in-law Samuel John were living in Karol Bagh, near where Pastor M. K. Chacko resided.

Sunday Morning Service

When Thomas told Timothy and the others at the Saturday meeting that he’d bring his family to the Sunday service as well, they were stunned. But he kept his word. The whole family came.

That Sunday morning, various members of the congregation stood and shared their testimonies. The Holy Spirit was already at work deep inside Thomas. He was so unsettled that he got up and walked outside three separate times during the service, only to come back in each time. After the testimonies, Pastor Chacko, aware that Thomas came from a Brethren Assembly background and sensing the visible change in him, looked in his direction and asked gently whether anyone else would like to share.

It was as if the Holy Spirit lifted him to his feet. Thomas stood up and cried out, “I am a sinner!” — and broke down weeping. The whole congregation wept with him and praised God through their tears.

On July 1, 1956, K. T. Thomas received the Lord Jesus Christ as his Savior. God, in His grace, had called him out of darkness and into the inheritance of the saints in light. Pastor Chacko came to him immediately, laid his hands on his head, and prayed over him. A wave of relief, joy, and freedom washed over Thomas unlike anything he had ever felt. The family walked home afterward full of gladness.

The God this man had hated had now filled him with overflowing joy. He could do nothing but praise and glorify Him. By four o’clock that same afternoon, he had already begun telling his friends about the Lord. The spiritual joy only grew with each passing day. The truth of 2 Corinthians 5:17 had become his own lived reality — “If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come.” The man who had lived without peace, at war with God and with himself, had been found, loved, and made a son.

From Inder Puri to Moti Bagh

Around this time, a government-owned house became available in Moti Bagh, and the family relocated from Inder Puri to A-219, Moti Bagh-I. K. D. Timothy and his family moved in with them at the same address. Those were full days: hours spent reading and meditating on the Word, praying together, visiting homes in the neighborhood with fellow believers, and witnessing to anyone who would listen.

Baptism

As Thomas studied Scripture and prayed, he came to understand clearly that baptism by immersion, in obedience to God’s counsel (Luke 7:30), the Lord’s command (Mark 16:16; Matt. 28:18–20), and the teaching and practice of the early church (Acts 2:38–42; 8:35–38), was the right and necessary step for those who had believed and been saved. Through baptism, a believer is united with Christ in His death, burial, and resurrection (Rom. 6:8; Gal. 3:27). With this conviction settled in his heart, Thomas went to Pastor Chacko and asked to be baptized.

On July 29, 1956, K. T. Thomas was baptized by Pastor M. K. Chacko. The entire day before his baptism he spent fasting and praying at the Faith Home. He came to the waters prepared, surrendered, and soaked in prayer.

He has since expressed sadness that this kind of deep preparation and conviction is so rarely seen in baptismal services today. Even so, he finds comfort in the knowledge that what is done in the Lord’s name and for the Lord’s sake is not done in vain. Because he had spent so many of his best years living entirely for the world, God now graciously stirred in him an urgent, energetic desire to give himself to the Lord’s work with everything he had.

Stepping into Ministry

Before long, Thomas began establishing and leading prayer groups in various locations across the city. His sister and her husband Samuel John, who shared his passion for the gospel, moved to Moti Bagh as well, and the two families worked side by side. Those prayer gatherings carried tangible spiritual power. At meeting after meeting, people were coming to faith.

For worship, Thomas attended the congregation in Karol Bagh where Pastor Chacko ministered. There he gave testimonies and exhortations, in Malayalam, as the Lord led him. Pastor Chacko would then translate it into Hindustani.2 When word reached his mother and family back home that he had been converted, their joy was overwhelming. They praised God with full hearts.

Pastor Chacko made regular visits from Karol Bagh to conduct Bible classes, and the work expanded steadily to include regular open-air meetings and gospel tract distribution throughout the area.

Entering the Lord’s Service

The First Malayalam Service in Delhi

The commute to Karol Bagh for worship was becoming a burden, so in October 1956, Thomas started holding services right in their home in Moti Bagh. It was a historic moment, the first Pentecostal worship service conducted in Malayalam in all of Delhi. Three families formed the core of that first gathering: Thomas and Marykutty, his brother-in-law Samuel John and family, and Timothy and family. A number of other Malayalam-speaking believers joined them as well.

The transformation in Thomas’s life had left his friends – both in the military and in civilian circles – genuinely stunned. That curiosity drew many of them to the prayer meetings, and through those gatherings, a number of them came to faith. One of them was a Hindu friend from the military named Kunchu, who came to visit one day. Thomas shared the gospel with him. Kunchu’s response was candid: “I can’t say I believe any of what you’re telling me. But what I can’t explain is the change I see in you.” Thomas took that as an opening and kept on witnessing.

Hindi Speakers Join In

As the home services and prayer meetings continued to bear fruit, something unexpected began happening: Hindi-speaking neighbors and acquaintances started showing up alongside the Malayalam community, and with equal enthusiasm. Thomas began preaching in Hindustani, and his brother-in-law Samuel John would translate into Malayalam. Of the three men at the center of the work, the author was the youngest in faith; and yet, by God’s extraordinary grace, it was he whom the others pressed forward to lead the ministry, and whom God equipped for it. Pastor Chacko came in from Karol Bagh to perform baptisms for those who were being saved through the ministry, and once a month he would come to administer Communion as well.

Pastor Chacko was more than a supervisor to Thomas; he was a genuine spiritual father in the work, someone who loved him deeply and cheered him on at every turn. His wife, as it happened, was a relative of Thomas’s mother. Under God’s grace, the ministry kept growing. New souls were added to the fellowship, and God’s name was glorified.

Pastor P. I. John

Pastor Chacko’s own ministry commitments – conventions and engagements in various places – made it increasingly difficult for him to give consistent support to the Moti Bagh congregation. He commended a fellow servant of the Lord who was then working in Shimla: Pastor P. I. John. In 1957, Pastor John took over the pastoral care of the Moti Bagh church. Then in 1958, he started a new work out of his own home in Tilak Nagar, and the Moti Bagh congregation naturally fell back to Thomas, who carried it forward from there.

At the time, Thomas was still employed at the VIP Guest House in Delhi. He spent his days witnessing without let-up, and held prayer meetings in the evenings. To make it all work, he consistently chose night shifts. The quieter pace of the office at night gave him stretches of time for prayer and Scripture-reading, and kept his days free for ministry.

The Kumbanad Convention

Early in 1957, Thomas and his family traveled home to Kerala. That year he attended the IPC General Convention in Kumbanad, where he met many of the senior leaders and servants of God in the IPC family. Though he had not grown up within the IPC, from that point forward he forged a bond with the organization and its founding generation that would never break. After the visit, he left his family behind in Kerala for a season and returned to Delhi.

Marykutty’s Illness and Healing

While the family was still in Kerala, Marykutty was struck by a severe case of typhoid. It came back a third time, and by the end she was so weakened that she hovered between life and death. A doctor came to the house, examined her, and told the family plainly that there was no hope, then left. Though she could not speak, she was still conscious enough to hear those words. In her heart she prayed: “God, You saved my husband who did not know You, and in your grace let him live as Your witness. Please give me a little more time to serve alongside him. Don’t hand me over to death now.”

Word reached Thomas in Delhi through Marykutty’s uncle. He had no way to get home quickly, so he prayed under the weight of it: “Lord, grant me the blessing of going home and finding my wife well.” A deep assurance settled in him that God had heard.

Marykutty herself has described what happened next, and in her own words:

“I was lying there facing death, praying in my heart, when I saw a terrifying figure, a man holding a sword, leaning over my face. Suddenly the sword dropped from his hand on its own. I knew in my spirit it was Satan, and I rebuked him: ‘Satan, leave me.’ I saw him go. Immediately, seven angels in white appeared at my bedside, some at my head, some at my feet, and I saw them lifting me up. I cried out in my heart, ‘Lord, are You taking me?’ Then I saw the angels gently lay me back down and stand there ministering to me. That very night, a pastor nearby was moved by the Holy Spirit to come to the house, and he laid his hands on my head and prayed. From that moment, I began to recover. I was nothing but skin and bones, utterly spent, but strength gradually returned. To God be the glory.”

Thomas came home soon after, found his wife alive and recovering, and praised God with a full heart.

Pastor T. N. Abraham

The late Pastor T. N. Abraham was a cousin of Thomas, and the news of his conversion and entry into ministry brought him enormous joy. He was one of Thomas’s earliest and most vocal encouragers in the Lord’s work. He once told him, “I won’t be able to come back to north India and work there anymore. Kochuunni, you must do the work I was meant to do as well.” Whenever Thomas visited Kerala, he would stay with Pastor T. N. Abraham in Kallissery. Pastor Abraham has since finished his race and now rests in the presence of the Lord he loved.

A Second Son

After Marykutty recovered, Thomas brought the family back to Delhi. The work was spreading to new areas of the city. Meetings were being held in the Cantonment area and in East Kidwai Nagar. On July 27, 1958, the Lord gave them another son. They gave him the pet name Saji, while his official name was registered as Benjamin Thomas.

Those were days of tireless, joyful labor. Thomas covered miles on his bicycle in the Lord’s service. In 1959, new souls kept coming to Christ. He poured himself into nurturing the young believers, encouraging them along the way. Prayer meetings happened nearly every day. He visited Safdarjung Hospital regularly, sitting with the sick, comforting the grieving, praying with them, and sharing the gospel. The nurses serving there in those years, many of them Christians, were enthusiastic participants in the services and midweek meetings. Several of them had a gift for evangelism and would bring their colleagues along. Many of those who came found the Lord and were baptized. God’s name was glorified.

As mentioned earlier, some Hindi-speaking friends had also found their way into the Moti Bagh fellowship. Among them was Shree Pal and his family, who participated faithfully and with great warmth. He is now himself serving as a pastor. Families from Nauroji Nagar and Krishna Nagar were also regular, wholehearted members of the congregation during those years.

Johny Ninan and Titus

A believer from Pathanapuram, Kerala, named Johny Ninan came to Delhi for work and ended up living with the Thomas family. His passion for the gospel was a real shot in the arm for the ministry. Thomas found his presence a genuine comfort. Johny Ninan later went on to serve as an officer with the Far East Broadcasting Association (FEBA) in Bangalore.

Around the same time, Thomas heard that C. P. Titus from Vadasserikara had come to Delhi and was living in Sarojini Nagar; but he had no address to track him down. One evening Thomas was walking through Sarojini Nagar market, praying quietly: “Lord, if only I could run into Titus.” A little further on, he spotted a man who looked Malayali. On an impulse he walked up and asked, “Is your name Titus?” The man said yes. Thomas burst into praise right there on the spot.

He invited him to the fellowship, and just like that, God had brought two men together who hadn’t even known they were in the same city. Titus threw himself into the gospel work with tremendous zeal. In 1967, he married the daughter of the late Pastor P. M. Samuel, and the family now lives in the United States.

Other faithful brothers and sisters who were part of the fellowship in those years included A. J. Chacko, P. E. Eapen, Jacob Thomas, Raj Purushottam, P. S. Samuel, Thankamma, Kunjamma, Kunjukunjamma, and Ammukutty.

Mrs. James’s Healing

By this point, the Moti Bagh congregation had grown into a genuinely diverse community–Hindi speakers, Malayalis, Tamilians, and Telugus worshiping together, with a significant contingent from the military.

On one occasion, Thomas brought Pastor Chacko along to a regular evening meeting at the military Cantonment. The wife of James, in whose home the meeting was held, had suffered from severe asthma for many years. The two servants of God prayed over her together and rebuked the sickness. The Lord healed her completely, on the spot, and His name was glorified. As a result, this dear couple, who had come from a Brethren Assembly background, began attending the Pentecostal fellowship at Moti Bagh. The asthma never returned. To God be all the glory.

The healings and the preaching of the Word continued to draw people in from the surrounding community. Those who were saved were baptized and added to the fellowship, with Pastor Chacko conducting the baptisms. But the baptisms also stirred up opposition. Some from within the mainline denominations who had been friendly toward Thomas turned hostile, and a few even moved to cause him trouble. One incident stands out.

A young man named Baby (Eapen), the grandson of a Mar Thoma elder named John upadesi from Poonaloor, had decided to be baptized. Some of his own family members, along with others from the Mar Thoma community, tried to stop the service. Pastor M. K. Chacko pressed ahead and conducted it without incident. Baby later married the daughter of Pastor T. G. Oommen and now lives with his family in Ernakulam, where he works.

A Daughter Is Born

After two sons, Thomas prayed specifically for a daughter. The Lord heard him. On August 15, 1961, a baby girl arrived: Suja (officially, Susan Thomas). Before the birth, Thomas had already told his friends he was expecting a girl. They pushed back: “No, it’ll be another boy.” The Lord honored His servant’s prayer and gave him exactly what he had asked for: a daughter.

Baptism of the Holy Spirit

Even while holding down a full-time government job, Thomas was pouring himself into the Lord’s work with everything he had; and souls were being won. Prayer meetings, open-air gatherings, tract distribution, person-to-person witness—he was operating like a full-time evangelist. And yet, through all of it, he had not been baptized in the Holy Spirit. Remarkably, no one who attended his meetings would have guessed it. The zeal and power evident in his ministry gave nothing away.

Pastor Chacko had spoken to him about the baptism of the Holy Spirit, but Thomas had been skeptical at first. As he kept pressing into the Word and prayer, however, God began building genuine faith in him for it. From that point on, his prayer became: “Lord, I hunger for every blessing You have promised in Your Word.”

He had, in fact, seen the reality of it years earlier. His mother and sister had both been baptized in the Spirit and spoken in languages they hadn’t learned. He remembered one particular moment from his school days when his younger sister, a girl who had no knowledge of English, was filled with the Spirit and began speaking clearly and fluently in English. That memory had stayed with him.

Receiving the Baptism of the Holy Spirit

Thomas sent his family back to Kerala for a season and set aside a full week for fasting and prayer. On a Friday evening, he went to a regular meeting at the Delhi Cantonment. Before the service even began, a fellow Christian pulled someone aside and said dismissively that there was no point in Thomas preaching since he hadn’t yet been baptized in the Spirit. He then walked out. When Thomas heard this, it cut him deeply.

After the meeting, he cycled to the office for his night shift. At an intersection along the way, weighed down with grief and longing, he stopped and prayed from the depths of his heart: “Lord, fill me with Your Spirit. If You don’t fill me before Sunday’s service, I will not say a single word. I will sit in silence.” He arrived at the office, got through the essential work, and kept praying through the night.

The next morning was Saturday. He took the day off, stayed home, and began praying early. His sister came over to pray with him, and another brother was there as well—just three people, no fanfare, no organized event, simply three believers in one accord in a home. Thomas shared what had happened the day before, and they prayed with focus and intention. Then they began to sing. Midway through the second song, before the verse was even finished, the Holy Spirit came upon Thomas with great power. He bounced around like an inflated ball.

For two full hours he spoke, sang, and worshiped in tongues. At one point he realized he had somehow moved to the other side of the room without knowing it. Parched with thirst, he tried to ask his sister for water but he could only speak in a foreign tongue. She couldn’t understand him until he finally gestured, and she brought him a drink. The joy he felt at that moment was beyond anything words could carry. His heart overflowed with praise. He marveled that the baptism of the Holy Spirit could bring such indescribable joy, and felt a pang of regret that he had approached it with skepticism for so long.

On October 14, 1961, in his own room, the Lord led His servant into the baptism of the Holy Spirit. To this day, Thomas speaks of it with gratitude and gives God the glory. From that point forward, he preached and taught on the Holy Spirit baptism with conviction and power, and over the years many people received this experience through his ministry, in Delhi and in every other place where God sent him.

An Extraordinary Vision

God had been giving Thomas glimpses of the work He intended to do through him in northern India. As he pressed on in ministry, one night the Lord granted him a vision unlike any he had received before.

He had spent the day and evening in the Lord’s service, as was his custom, and arrived at the office for his night shift. After finishing the essential work, he prayed and lay down to rest. The Lord met him in an extraordinary way. The vision began after eleven at night and continued until five-thirty in the morning.

What he saw was nothing less than a panoramic view of what God was going to accomplish through his life. His full-time ministry, the breadth and reach of the work ahead, a life of radical faith and complete dependence on God, trials as fierce as a furnace. The Lord showed him all of it in advance. In the years that followed, every part of that vision came to pass: he left his job, the work expanded, he walked through valley after valley of tears, he passed through the fire. Through it all, a God who never let go held him steady, supplied his every need, great and small, and brought him through to blessing and deliverance. It still strikes him as nothing short of wonderful.

Our God is One who reveals His ways to His children ahead of time (Psalm 25:14; 103:7; Genesis 18:17–18; Daniel 2:28).

Thomas testifies confidently that from that night to this, he has not turned aside from the path of faith God laid out before him. He still depends on God alone for every daily need. Because God showed him the road in advance, including the rocks and thorns along the way, he has been able to face hardship without being crushed by it, always returning to God as his refuge and source of strength.

Suffering, he has learned, is not meant to break a child of God. It is meant to build them. The paths of trial walked by the faithful have always served as a beacon for the generations that follow. Who reads the story of Job and does not come away comforted? The psalms David wrote in the midst of anguish and sorrow have been a source of consolation for him and for generations beyond counting. And it was John, suffering on Patmos, who was caught up into heavenly visions and left the church a detailed account of what God’s plans. Through the rough terrain of his Christian life, Thomas has found that a faith tested and proved produces a witness that shines. He rests on the promise: “I will never leave you nor forsake you.” That God has been utterly faithful.

Dear reader, place your trust in this God. Let Him make your life a source of blessing.

A Daughter’s Brush with Death

In March 1962, little Suja fell seriously ill with what appeared to be an abdominal condition. Twenty days passed with no improvement. The congregation prayed for her healing every day. As her condition deteriorated, the brothers urged Thomas to take her to the hospital. He held back, determined to keep pressing God in prayer.1

Then one night, her breathing stopped—twice. Marykutty cried out through tears: “Lord, at least wait until her father comes home from work to take her.”

Time moved slowly. When Thomas came home in the morning, Marykutty told him everything through tears. Suja was barely alive. He went straight to his prayer room, weeping, and spent the next two hours in prayer and meditation on the Word. The door to the room was left slightly ajar.

After about two hours, Marykutty stepped in from the kitchen to check on the baby and found the bed soaked with a yellow fluid that had come from Suja’s abdomen. She scooped the baby up and rushed to the bathroom. As she turned on the tap and the water ran over the child, Suja’s head and limbs went completely limp, slipping out of her grasp. “My baby, my baby!” she cried, and pushed through the half-open door into Thomas’s room.

“Don’t cry,” he said, and took the child quickly into his own arms.

Together they knelt, and prayed with broken hearts: “Lord, You gave her to us as a gift. You have the right to take her back. But Lord, we love her. You have the power to restore her life. You are the God who raised Lazarus when he had been dead four days and his body was already decomposing. Lord, restore the life of this child.”

As they prayed, Thomas felt the faintest movement in the child’s body. He opened his eyes. Something had changed. Suja’s eyes were opening, and she seemed to be looking up at her father.

It was, even to Thomas and Marykutty themselves, almost too astonishing to believe. They wept tears of joy and praised the Lord together. “This child will not die,” Thomas said, and placed her gently back in Marykutty’s arms.

They remembered the words of Psalm 23, “Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil,” and praised God together. The congregation was stunned. The glory went to God.

Pappachan’s Healing

Around the same time, a brother named Pappachan from Poonaloor, who was staying with Thomas and was the younger brother of Geevarughese, a co-minister at the Bhopal church, was struck by a severe case of ascites, an extreme buildup of fluid in the abdomen. He was a tailor by trade, but only a few weeks after arriving in Delhi, the illness had overtaken him. He was a man of deep prayer, committed to regular fasting, and the three brothers – Thomas, Titus, and Eapen – formed a close-knit prayer group bound together in real spiritual unity.

As his abdomen swelled uncontrollably, he had to be carried on his cot from the prayer room into an adjoining room during meetings. The sisters who worked at Safdarjung Hospital assessed the situation plainly: “There is no cure for this condition. We can admit him to the hospital so he can at least rest comfortably and receive care.” Neither Pappachan nor Thomas wanted to go that route, but eventually they relented, and he was admitted to Safdarjung Hospital. Fluid was drained from his abdomen twice, but the condition returned and worsened. He was failing.

Thomas was fasting and interceding urgently for both Pappachan’s healing and his daughter’s recovery when the Lord gave him a specific vision: he saw himself standing at Pappachan’s bedside, placing his hand on the swollen abdomen and praying; and as he did, the swelling gradually subsiding. He got up from prayer immediately, changed his clothes, and went straight to the hospital. Visiting hours had just begun. He went directly to Pappachan, placed his hand on his abdomen, prayed, and left, trusting the Lord to deliver.

On his way out, he ran into an elder of a Brethren Assembly who worked in the hospital’s blood bank. They got to talking about Pappachan’s condition. The man shook his head: “Poor soul. He’s going to die.” Thomas replied quietly but firmly: “Kuriachan, our Lord is able to heal him. Please don’t talk like that.” The man scoffed, asking whether anyone, anywhere in five continents, had ever been healed through prayer from this disease. Thomas reminded him that even when his own mother and sister were Brethren believers, before they had any Pentecostal convictions about divine healing, the Lord had healed them of serious conditions without any medicine. “God will most certainly heal Pappachan. Even if you see it with your own eyes, you still won’t believe it.” Kuriachan walked away irritated.

The next day Thomas went back to the hospital. And there was Pappachan, sitting up, the swelling visibly and dramatically reduced. The two men were overcome with joy and wept together, glorifying God. Within three or four days, Pappachan was discharged and came home.

In the wake of these miracles – his daughter raised back from the edge of death, his brother healed of an incurable disease – the congregation kept pouring out praise to God. And through it all, the Holy Spirit began to press upon Thomas in a new and distinct way. The Lord was calling him to resign from his job and enter full-time Christian ministry. These events had only deepened his faith and made that call impossible to ignore.

On July 28, 1962, the Lord gave Thomas and Marykutty another son. They named him Joji (officially, Samuel Thomas).

Full-Time Ministry

By late 1962, the home they had been using for worship had become too small for the growing congregation. Thomas moved both the family and the services to K-12, South Extension Part I, New Delhi. The apartment back in Motibagh was taken over by Johny Ninan and a few other brothers.

Pastor P. M. Samuel Comes to Delhi

The year 1963 brought several turning points. The Holy Spirit had been pressing Thomas for some time to resign from his government job, and he had finally drafted the letter. Around that same time, the late Pastor P. M. Samuel came to Delhi and stayed with the family for a stretch. Thomas felt led by the Spirit to hand the resignation letter to Pastor Samuel and ask him to pray over it.

Pastor Samuel’s response was not what Thomas had hoped for. He advised Thomas to keep doing what he was already doing, serving the Lord while holding down his government position. Then, to drive the point home, he pressed Thomas with seven pointed questions, each one aimed at talking him out of resigning.

Thomas held his ground. “The Lord told me to leave,” he said. “No matter how many questions you ask, nothing is going to change that. Please, just lay your hands on this letter and pray over it.”

Pastor Samuel paused. Then he said something candid and worth remembering. “When Oonnoonni saar,1 K. C. Cherian, and I first went out into gospel work, if we had eight annas2 between us, the three of us split it equally. But times have changed. Even if I have money in my pocket, I may not be there for you when you really need it.”

Thomas received that honestly and replied without flinching. “Appachan,3 the Lord is the one who called me. Whatever you say, I am not turning back. Please just pray over this letter.” Pastor Samuel pulled him close, wept, prayed over the letter, blessed him with the words, “May the Lord help you, brother,” and handed it back.

Resigning from the Job

Thomas submitted the resignation to his office. Shortly afterward, his administrative officer, a Hindu Bengali man to whom Thomas had previously shared the gospel, called him in and asked why he was leaving. Thomas told him plainly: he was setting aside all his time for the service of Jesus Christ.

The officer pushed back. “You’re already doing that work on the side. Why give up a permanent government job? You have housing, a steady income. What will you do without all that?” When none of this moved Thomas, the officer tried one more angle. “You have eighteen years of service. Stay two more years and you’ll qualify for a pension. Think of your wife and children.”

Thomas had his answer ready. “If thirty days of service to the Government of India earns me a paycheck on the first day of the following month, then the God I serve will do vastly more.”

The resignation was accepted, though the actual release from service came a few months later.

Obedience to the Spirit’s leading brought Thomas a deep, settled peace and joy. When the final payment from his years of service came through, he spent the bulk of it on gospel work. There was no organizational backing at the time, no promises of support, no safety net. Like Abraham, the father of the faithful, Thomas stepped out with nothing but his trust in God. He has said since that the ministers who have proven most useful to the Lord are the ones who stepped out looking at God’s face rather than any human face.

The years that followed were not easy. There were seasons of genuine financial strain, times when the family went without enough food, moments when Thomas cried out to God, crushed at heart. But the Lord never let go. He never left Thomas suffer humiliation. He made a way, every time, right up to the present day.

A Hindu Is Healed

During this period, the Lord worked several remarkable healings through Thomas, and His name was glorified. A Hindi-speaking Hindu friend who lived in East Kidwai Nagar had been struck by paralysis from the waist down. The hospitals gave up on him, and he was sent home without hope. Some believing sisters who had cared for him at the hospital brought his situation to Thomas’s attention. Thomas went to visit him, along with a few brothers and sisters from the congregation. They shared the gospel with him and prayed, then left.

When they went back a few days later, the man was noticeably better. He had begun sitting up on his own. They visited again, shared the gospel, prayed, and encouraged him to live for the Lord. Gradually, he recovered completely.

A Relative Is Healed

Around the same time, a maternal uncle of Marykutty had been bedridden for several months, unable to sit up or even turn his head. Thomas had visited him before, shared the gospel, urged him to commit his life to God, and prayed for him. But despite visits to multiple hospitals, the doctors had never been able to diagnose his condition. He lay at home in Lakshmibai Nagar, without answers or hope.

One day his wife called and asked Thomas to come and pray. Thomas remembered that the gospel had been shared with this man more than once and that he had not yet responded. He told her directly: if her husband was willing to put his full trust in the Lord and commit to living for God, Thomas would come and pray. Three days later she called again. “We believe. Please come.”

Thomas went, along with Marykutty and a sister named Rosamma, who had spent years doing gospel work in Andhra alongside the late Pastor P. M. Samuel and was staying with them in Delhi at the time. For close to an hour, Thomas spoke the gospel to the family, who were Mar Thoma believers. At the end, he read aloud from James the passage about anointing the sick with oil and praying over them.

Before anyone asked for anything, the lady of the house got up quietly and came back from the kitchen with a small dish of oil. Thomas and the others were struck by it. Thomas had never anointed anyone with oil before, and the woman had had no prior knowledge of the practice or any reason to expect it. Thomas turned to Sister Rosamma and asked what he should do. Her answer was simple: “Follow God’s leading, Thomachan.”

Thomas turned to the man in the bed. “Do you believe that the Lord can save you and heal you?” From where he lay, the man answered, “I believe.”

Thomas took the oil, prayed over it, anointed both the man’s shoulders, prayed again, and commanded healing in the name of Jesus. What happened next startled everyone in the room. The man cried out “Hallelujah!” for the first time in his life, leaped out of bed, and walked back and forth across the room, praising God!

Everyone present broke into praise together. Not long after, the woman of the house came to faith and was baptized.

An Act of Kindness

In those days, Titus, Thomas’s younger brother George K. Thomas, and a relative named K. T. Benny were all living with the family. One morning around eleven, after the other brothers had left for their offices, someone knocked at the gate. Thomas went down to find a young man standing there in rough, worn clothing, his hair long and unkempt. He was looking for work and asked if anything could be arranged.

There was something about his face, a quiet joy that was hard to place, and it caught Thomas’s attention. Thomas told him honestly that he was a Christian missionary with no position to offer anyone.

The young man said, “All right, sir,” and turned to leave.

Thomas called after him: “Wait. Come inside first. Have something to eat before you go.” They were speaking in English.

“No, sir, I’ll be on my way,” the man said, but Thomas opened the gate himself, gently insisted, and led him to the prayer room.

Thomas offered him a chair; the man chose to sit on the folded mat on the floor instead. Thomas stepped inside and told Marykutty they had a guest. She came out, took one look at him, and went back to the kitchen without a word.

Over the next little while, the young man unfolded his story. He had grown up in a Hindu family in northern Bihar.1 His father died while he was still in college, and the weight of family responsibility fell on him. He had gone to Calcutta looking for work, found nothing, and made his way to Delhi. For the past several nights, the railway station had been his bed.

Thomas shared briefly about God’s love and care. Somewhere in the middle of it, the young man fell asleep. When the meal was ready, Thomas woke him gently and insisted he eat with them. The man was visibly taken aback by the welcome, and praying before the meal was something he had never encountered. That someone would treat a man who had come to the door looking like a beggar with such ordinary warmth left him genuinely astonished.

After the meal, Thomas shared the gospel with him, grounding it in the love of God. He told the young man that God had brought him to that door, that the family had treated him the way they had because they loved God, and that God’s love for sinful humanity was far greater than anything they could show. Thomas gave him clothing and other things he needed, and from that point on welcomed him as one of the household.

At a Wednesday Bible class not long after, when Thomas gave an invitation to believe in Jesus and receive salvation, he sensed in his spirit that something had shifted in this young man’s heart. But the man didn’t come forward publicly. Thomas went away for meetings elsewhere shortly after, having made arrangements for the young man’s needs in his absence. After a few days, the young man quietly took stock of the household’s circumstances and decided he didn’t want to add to Thomas’s burden. He thanked the family and left for Bombay.

Years passed. The young man faded from Thomas’s memory.

Then in 1972, during the IPC Northern Region Convention in the city of Patna, in Bihar, Thomas had finished a morning Bible class for ministers and was resting in his room when a visitor arrived, a north Indian man accompanied by someone from the Patna congregation. He introduced himself with a phrase that stopped Thomas and Marykutty cold: “I am your grandson.”

He then explained. He had been a Naxalite militant.2 He had killed people. One day, walking through the streets of Calcutta,3 he noticed a crowd and wandered over. A man was standing and preaching about Jesus Christ, about the free salvation God had provided for all of humanity through Christ, about peace for those who had none. His own life had been without peace for as long as he could remember. He surrendered it to Jesus, the Prince of Peace. He confessed everything he had done, with tears and a broken heart. And the miracle happened: Jesus Christ became his Savior. A life that had been headed toward destruction was filled with the joy of heaven. He had been saved. He had become a son of God.

The man who had led him to Christ on that Calcutta street was the same young man Thomas and his family had taken in off the street in Delhi in 1963: Pastor J. P. Gupta. “That is how I became your grandson.”

Thomas, Marykutty, and everyone in the room praised God together.

After leaving Delhi, Guptaji4 had come to faith in Bombay, been baptized, received some training for ministry, and gone out as an evangelist. And it was that young evangelist, the one Thomas had once fed and clothed and housed without any expectation of return, who had stood on a Calcutta street and brought the gospel to a hardened, violent young man who desperately needed it.

The story didn’t end there. In February 1988, Thomas preached one evening at the IPC Northern Region East District Convention in Patna. After the message, a man came running up to him. “Do you know who I am?” Thomas apologized and said he didn’t.

The man said: “Twenty-five years ago, in 1963, I came to your gate like a homeless man. You welcomed me like a brother. I am J. P. Gupta.”

Thomas embraced him and called out, “My brother!”

They prayed together and praised God. The Word of God had proven true once more: “Your labor in the Lord is not in vain” (1 Corinthians 15:58). Every act done in the name of the Lord carries its own fruit, in ways and in times we cannot foresee.

Pastor J. P. Gupta now ministers at Darbhanga in north Bihar. May the Lord continue to bless him and make him a blessing to many. And may the Lord give each reader the same grace to reach out to the lost and the desperate with the love of God.

Ministry Growth and Ordination

By the end of 1963, the Lord had opened doors for gospel work in several areas surrounding Delhi. The most significant of these were Faridabad in Haryana, Ghaziabad in Uttar Pradesh, and Mehrauli in Delhi itself. Faridabad and Ghaziabad would eventually become home to established congregations. Through all of this, Thomas continued holding prayer meetings and conducting open-air evangelism every day of the week.

The Delhi Church Convention

That year’s annual convention for the Delhi congregation ran from October 28 through November 3, held at Thomas’s residence in South Extension. The speakers included Pastor P. M. Samuel, Pastor M. K. Chacko, a senior Assemblies of God missionary working in northern India, and the principal of the Hardoi Bible School. Ten students from the Hardoi Bible School also attended the convention.

Thomas’s Ordination

On November 3, during the worship service, Thomas was ordained as a minister by the servants of God gathered there. The primary role in the ordination service was carried out by the senior statesman of the IPC, Pastor P. M. Samuel.

The Lord moved powerfully throughout the convention. Many came to faith, and several were baptized. Among those baptized was Pastor P. M. Samuel’s youngest son, Johny (John Samuel), who now lives with his family in Canada, walking joyfully with the Lord. He and Mathews from Detroit, USA, visited Thomas together with their families not long ago.

A Vision After the Ordination

Thomas has described in his own words a vision he received shortly after his ordination:

“After praying for a long time, until about two-thirty in the morning, I lay down on a cot beneath the stairs of the house where I was living. The Lord gave me a vision. I saw myself standing in a valley surrounded by mountains on every side.
When I looked up, I saw crowds descending from all four directions, coming straight at me, armed with sticks, stones, and knives. I cried out, ’lord, is this the end of my life?’
From behind me came a voice: ‘Are you afraid of what you see?’
I answered, ’lord, if this is Your will, I am ready to die this way.’
The voice came again: ’son, the stones and the knives and the sticks in the hands of those people will leave their marks on your body, one by one. But for every one of those marks, I have prepared a reward for you.’
I said, ’thank You, Lord,’ and opened my eyes. I was filled with the Spirit and praised the Lord.”

It was a vision of the great sufferings that lay ahead of him in the years to come.

Having left his government work to serve the Lord fully, Thomas walked forward on the path of faith. Resigning from his job meant giving up his government quarters as well, and the testing of that faith-life began almost immediately. Unable to keep up with the rent on the South Extension house, he had to let it go.

The Lord’s Care in Hard Times

As 1964 began, the trials pressed harder. Thomas found a small two-room house far from the center of the city, with few basic amenities, at a reduced rent. Even so, daily needs were a constant strain. Two of his children were in school. When the school holidays came, he decided to send the family back to Kerala to ease the burden, but he had no money for the train tickets. At the time, a single fare from Delhi to Kerala was thirty-eight rupees.

He was sitting with this weight on him when a telegram arrived from Pastor P. M. Samuel: he was coming to Delhi on urgent government business and needed help with some official matters. Thomas went to the airport to receive him. When Pastor Samuel arrived, Thomas told him honestly that the house was not fit to accommodate a guest. Pastor Samuel brushed it off: “It doesn’t matter, brother. This is the faith life, isn’t it? I’ll manage at a hotel.” He stayed at the Marina Hotel in Connaught Place.

The next morning, the two men went together to meet a government minister and sorted out the official business. Afterward, they returned to the hotel, knelt together, and prayed at length, both of them in tears. Thomas mentioned that he was planning to send his family to Kerala. When it was time to say goodbye, Pastor Samuel quietly slipped something into Thomas’s pocket. On the bus ride home, Thomas reached in and found a hundred-rupee note. Standing in that crowded bus, he wept with gratitude and praised God. A hundred rupees was exactly enough for his wife’s and children’s train tickets and the necessary travel expenses. How good God is.

When Thomas got home, the brothers were gathered and praying. Titus was mid-prayer when Thomas walked in: “Lord, Your servant’s wife and children need to travel home tomorrow. Please open the way.” Thomas knelt and added his own prayer: “Lord, thank You for hearing and answering.” After prayers, Titus turned to him: “Pastor, did the Lord work? Did He open the way?” Thomas told him what had happened, and they all praised God together.

The next morning, as Thomas saw his family off, he said to Marykutty with quiet emotion, “When the Lord opens a way and provides us a proper home to live in, I’ll come and bring you back.”

Seven Days of Fasting

After the family left, Thomas set aside seven days for fasting and prayer. The rent at the time was one hundred rupees a month, and three months had gone unpaid. He brought all of it before God, crying out in his need, day after day.

During one of those fasting days, he was praying with a Hindi-speaking brother when both of them were taken up in the Spirit. Thomas became aware that the brother was speaking to him in tongues, delivering what he understood to be a prophetic word. Weeping, Thomas asked the Lord what He was saying to him in his helplessness. At that moment, he saw a vision: a Bible lying open to the beginning of Isaiah 45, with verse 3 clearly visible, and the page turning to show the passage that followed. When he came out of the vision, he opened his Bible to that same place and found exactly what he had seen, clear and unmistakable.

The Lord was speaking to him through it: the bronze doors and iron bars standing in the way of the ministry would be broken, and God would lead him through in triumph. Thomas took courage.

In the difficult days that followed, that word became a steadying anchor. When he felt close to collapse, it kept drawing his eyes back to God rather than to the circumstances closing in around him. Through it all, he came to know for himself something he would carry for the rest of his life: God does not allow His children to be broken or crushed. He holds them.

A Macedonian Call to Jamnagar

The fasting and prayer continued. The day after the events of the previous chapter, Thomas was deep in prayer when the landlady knocked to say there was a phone call from Jamnagar,1 Gujarat. He went to take it. On the line were K. A. George and Rajappan, two Air Force personnel from Delhi who were on temporary duty there.

They explained the situation. A small prayer group had been quietly meeting at the Presbyterian church in Jamnagar, made up of three local believers and a handful of others from the area. When K. A. George and Rajappan showed up, the group came alive, and before long they were eager to organize a proper series of meetings. They had contacted several people, including M. E. Cherian, editor of the magazine Evangelist, but nobody had taken them up on the invitation. So they thought of Pastor Thomas, who had just stepped away from his government job to pursue full-time ministry. Would he come for four days?

Thomas said yes, but with one condition: the brothers had to still be there when he arrived, since he didn’t know a soul in Jamnagar. The meetings were set for April. K. A. George and his family now live in New York.

Back in prayer, Thomas pressed into God hard. He put two specific requests on the table, two signs that would confirm he was being sent by God and that God intended him to minister there long-term. The first: God would have to do a miracle on the very day he arrived. The second: even with such a small group, seven souls would come to faith and be baptized through his ministry. If both happened, Thomas told God, he would take it as clear confirmation. He set out for Jamnagar in April, leaving Titus to hold things together in Delhi.

He arrived on the appointed day to find both brothers waiting at the railway station. Since it was already past eleven, they suggested grabbing a meal at a nearby South Indian restaurant before heading to the camp. Thomas asked why they weren’t going to a believer’s home. They explained: the only Pentecostal family at the Air Force station was Kuriachan and his wife, and their only son had taken seriously ill and been admitted to the hospital, so the plan for Thomas’s meals had been arranged around their home. The group ate and reached the camp around noon. Though the child hadn’t improved much, the Kuriachan family had insisted on getting him discharged so they could be home to welcome the servant of God.

A Miracle in Jamnagar

Kuriachan’s immediate neighbor was a Brethren Assembly member named John, from Puthenkavu, whose family happened to be back in Kerala. His house had been set aside for Thomas to rest in. That afternoon Thomas was alone in the room, praying for God’s blessing on the evening meeting, when he thought he heard someone call out, “Brother.” He stayed in prayer. Then it came again: “Brother, Brother.”

Assuming it was the sister next door, he got up, opened the door, and said, “What is it, Sister?”

Kuriachan’s little boy had taken a sharp turn for the worse and lost consciousness. His mother was at the door, crying out in desperation, pacing, confessing her sins aloud. Thomas went straight in and prayed over the child. As he did, the prayer he had prayed back in Delhi came rushing back to him. He interceded with everything he had, asking God to do the miracle He had promised. The child jolted awake.

Thomas said quietly, “Sister, just believe. The Lord has healed him,” then went back to his room and kept praying.

A short while later she returned to the door: “Brother, the Lord has healed him completely. He’s up and walking around.” The first sign had come to pass, exactly as Thomas had prayed. He praised God, and when the brothers gathered that evening, they joined him in glorifying this wonder-working Lord.

The Gospel Meetings in Jamnagar

The four-day series was a genuine blessing. People from other churches and denominations in the area came with curiosity and kept returning. Exactly seven people came to faith, precisely the number Thomas had asked God for, and every one of them decided to be baptized according to the Word.

It is worth pausing here to note that Thomas had never conducted a baptismal service himself. Up to this point he had always brought in Pastor Chacko for both baptisms and the Lord’s Supper. Pastor Chacko had actually been pressing Thomas for a while to take on these responsibilities himself, given that his calling to ministry was unmistakable; but Thomas had kept deferring to him.

Looking back on this, Thomas offers a word of pastoral caution. He has noticed, with some concern, that young brothers sent out for ministry today sometimes get “Pastor” on a business card before they’ve been genuinely seasoned, then rush to conduct baptisms, Communion, and even weddings, bypassing more experienced ministers who are right there and available. His concern isn’t about authority; it’s about the integrity of the ministry. His counsel is simple: wait patiently until the Lord opens the door. Romans 12:3 is worth sitting with: “Do not think of yourself more highly than you ought.”

Baptism in the Arabian Sea

Jamnagar sits in the Saurashtra region, and its dry climate meant there was no suitable body of water nearby for a baptismal service. After some discussion, the brothers settled on the Gulf of Kutch, an inlet of the Arabian Sea. A truck was arranged to take the group to the coast. A few people raised concerns about entering the sea, but Thomas prayed through it, trusted God, and the Lord gave him grace to carry out the service without a single problem.

The first to be baptized was Sister Mariamma, a Mar Thomite. She came up out of the water visibly renewed in the Spirit. Nobody present that day ever forgot it. This, everyone sensed, was what real baptism looked like. All seven were baptized, just as Thomas had prayed. Praise God.

The Sunday Service and the Lord’s Supper

The Sunday morning service included the Lord’s Supper, the first time Thomas had ever administered Communion. Twelve participated at the table, Thomas among them. Visitors from other churches had come as well, curious to see what a Pentecostal worship service looked like. The gathering was held under a canopy set up among the Air Force quarters, and it was deeply blessed. Believers and non-believers sat through the entire service in tears.

A brief theological note belongs here. The Lord’s Supper is not merely a tradition. Jesus commanded it (Luke 22:19-20), the early church both taught and practiced it (Acts 2:41-42; 20:7, 11), and the epistles explain its full meaning and weight (1 Corinthians 10:16-17; 11:23-32). Those who come to the table, those who have been saved and baptized, made partakers of Christ’s death, look backward to a crucified Savior, forward to a returning King, and inward to examine themselves honestly. It is a profoundly sacred ordinance, bound to the death of Christ at Calvary. The church must observe it with the reverence it deserves, and without a trace of casualness.

After the service, a Catholic friend came to Thomas in tears. “Pastor, because I haven’t been baptized, I couldn’t take part in this blessed service.”

Then a brother named Matthaichan from Mavelikkara, who worked at DIGJAM (Digvijaya Woolen Mills), also came forward weeping. “Pastor, twenty-four years ago I was baptized in Bombay by Oonnoonni saar (Pastor K. E. Abraham2). But I became a backslider afterward. Will the Lord forgive me? Will He take me back? Can I still be part of this fellowship?” Thomas told him plainly that the Lord forgives every sin when someone comes with a repentant heart. Matthaichan agreed then and there, with tears, to come back to God.

In the years that followed, Matthaichan threw himself into the growth of the Jamnagar church with tireless devotion. Later, when Thomas came to Jamnagar with his family for an extended ministry season and they went through real hunger and hardship, it was Matthaichan who loved them, served them, and stood by them through all of it. Thomas speaks of it to this day with deep gratitude and prays that the Lord will reward him and his family richly. Matthaichan now lives near Mavelikkara, in Kurathikad.

Back in Delhi: an Unexpected Crisis

Before leaving Jamnagar the next morning, Thomas told the brothers he planned to return within two months to take up the ongoing ministry there, and asked them to find a house to rent in the meantime. He got back to Delhi in the last week of April.

The moment he walked in, the landlady hit him with news: three months of rent were overdue, and she needed the money and the house immediately.

With practically nothing in his pocket, Thomas was in a bind. He gathered what he could, a pedestal fan and a few household items, and handed them over to her for two hundred and seventy rupees, promising to bring the remaining thirty shortly. She told him to forget the thirty and just get out as soon as possible.

He packed their belongings with no idea where they would go. Within a week, God made a way, as He had a way of doing, and they were out and settled somewhere new. How the Lord provided that next place was a story in itself.

Sister Nathaniel

A man named Mr. Nathaniel had served as a Deputy Secretary in the Government of India until he died of a heart attack while still in service. His widow was a member of Pastor Chacko’s congregation in Karol Bagh. After his death, the government gave her special permission to run a junior school in the bungalow she occupied in Motibagh, and two of Thomas’s children were students there. This Hindi-speaking sister had a genuine hunger for spiritual things. She worshiped regularly at Pastor Chacko’s church, but also participated in Thomas’s midweek meetings and special services, and had often put her car at his disposal for ministry needs.

One of the sisters in Thomas’s congregation happened to mention in passing to Sister Nathaniel that Pastor Thomas was being forced out of his place in Krishna Nagar because he couldn’t pay the rent. The very next morning, she sent her car for him.

When Thomas asked the driver, a man from north India, what this was about, all he got was: “Memsaab3 wants to see you.”

When Thomas arrived, she asked him directly what was going on and why he was moving. He said nothing, just stayed quiet. She stood up, and they drove together to the building in South Extension II where her school was. She walked him through it, showed him rooms that could work for worship and for living, pointed out the kitchen, and then said: “Pastor, come and stay here. No rent required.”

Thomas’s eyes filled with tears. He praised God, quietly overwhelmed, remembering the vision God had given him when he had cried out not knowing where to turn. The Lord had prepared the way, just as He had shown him He would. They moved in shortly afterward and continued holding services and prayer meetings right there.

Sister Nathaniel Is Transformed

One day during this period, Sister Nathaniel came to one of Thomas’s church services. Prompted by the Spirit, Thomas preached on holiness, and the message carried real weight.

Sister Nathaniel, a north Indian woman accustomed to wearing a great deal of jewelry, felt deeply convicted. After the service she came to Thomas quietly but with real emotion. “Pastor Thomas, I have never heard this teaching before in my life. The Lord has been working in my heart, and I have decided to remove all my jewelry.” She followed through on her word.

Emmanuel’s Second Baptism

Sister Nathaniel, along with a north Indian brother named Emmanuel and several others, was a regular at the prayer meetings. Emmanuel attended services, shared testimonies, and took Communion. One day he told Thomas he wanted to be baptized. Thomas was puzzled and asked him to explain.

Emmanuel said: “Pastor, I was a Baptist church member. In their practice, when children come of age they are baptized by immersion, but the emphasis is on the act itself, not necessarily on a prior conversion experience. I was baptized that way, before I had genuine faith. Now I am a person who has assurance of salvation, so I need to be baptized. And I also know I need to be baptized before I can receive the baptism of the Holy Spirit.”

Thomas baptized him according to the Word. At the very next prayer meeting, he received the baptism of the Holy Spirit.

Sister Nathaniel Receives the Holy Spirit

The special prayer meetings continued, and several people were filled with the Spirit during that season. Then one day, right in the middle of a prayer meeting, Sister Nathaniel herself was filled with the Holy Spirit. She entered a new depth of joy and spiritual growth that marked her life from that point on.

Reconnecting and Expanding

Thomas stayed in regular contact with the Jamnagar congregation by correspondence. Meanwhile, Timothy, who had shared the Motibagh house with Thomas in the early years before transferring to Andhra Pradesh, came back to Delhi after seven years away. Thomas asked him to work alongside Titus in the Delhi church, and agreed to return every three months to handle the key ministry responsibilities himself.

Meeting Stephen

The week before his Jamnagar trip, Thomas went to Karol Bagh to see Pastor Chacko, only to find him away at a convention. That same day, Stephen from Gandhidham in the Kutch region happened to be there. He had come to let Pastor Chacko know that a small Pentecostal congregation had formed in Gandhidham and was looking for someone to minister to them.

Thomas introduced himself, and when Stephen heard the name K. T. Thomas, he mentioned that he had recently heard about a K. T. Thomas who had conducted a wonderfully blessed series of meetings in Jamnagar. Thomas confirmed that he was the same person, and that he was heading back to Jamnagar the very next week. Stephen immediately invited him to visit Gandhidham as well.

Thomas said he would think it over once he got to Jamnagar. They exchanged addresses, prayed together, and parted. Stephen couldn’t travel with him since he needed to go to Dehradun first. He has since left his job and now serves as the minister of the Church of Gandhidham, an independent congregation.

Journey to Gujarat

As planned, Thomas traveled to Jamnagar at the start of July. The region had long been ruled by a royal family with a well-known hostility toward Christians, and no Christian missionary had ever been granted permission to work there. The brothers had done everything they could to find a house suitable for meetings and residence, but nothing had come through. Thomas moved in with Matthaichan from the Woolen Mills and began pastoring the congregation from there. By this point Matthaichan had been spiritually restored and was walking steadily with the Lord.

A House in the Palace of a King Who Hated Christians

With no house available, Thomas decided to take the matter straight to God through fasting. The day after the fast began, he and Mathews, one of the Air Force personnel he had baptized earlier, walked along the main road near Irwin Hospital, the city’s principal medical center, and began asking around about a large house they had noticed there. (Mathews is a nephew of Isaac saar from Poonaloor and now lives with his family in Kuwait.)

The house, they discovered, belonged to the elder brother of a former royal official of Jamnagar. Nearby stood another large property, equally imposing. When they asked about that one, they were told it belonged to the king himself, and that the crown prince might be willing to negotiate.

A large iron gate stood at the front of that property. Thomas remembered the promise God had given him during his fasting days in Delhi, the one about iron bars and bronze doors. He prayed specifically that the Lord would open them, then walked up and introduced himself to the crown prince as a Christian missionary looking for suitable premises for gospel work.

The prince looked at him. “Are you Malayali? I like Malayalis.” He came down from the upper floor and showed Thomas two large rooms on the ground level, along with a kitchen. “Come back tomorrow if you’re interested,” he said.

Thomas had gone in expecting to be asked for at least five hundred rupees a month. Instead, the next morning the prince told him simply: “Pay a hundred rupees a month to my mother. That’s all.” Thomas could barely take it in.

He walked away praising the God who makes the impossible possible and holds all of time in His hands. Sajjan Nivas, the royal house of a king who had shut his doors to Christianity, had just been opened for the proclamation of the gospel of Christ. How it happened remains a wonder to everyone who knew about it.

Ministry in Gandhidham

After settling into Jamnagar, Thomas traveled to Gandhidham as Stephen had requested. The brothers there had arranged a five-day series of meetings. The messages were Spirit-filled and carried real weight, and the Lord’s Supper was observed as well. The congregation was so blessed that they asked Thomas unanimously to become their pastor. He agreed, on the condition that he be free to visit Delhi once every three months and serve in Jamnagar for two weeks of every month. They accepted.

With that, the Lord entrusted His servant with the pastoral care of three congregations at the same time: Delhi, Jamnagar, and Gandhidham. Gandhidham sat on the northern shore of the Gulf of Kutch and Jamnagar on the southern, making the road route between them long and roundabout. But a short air service connected them directly: twenty rupees and twenty minutes by plane. The Almighty arranged for Thomas to make that flight four times a month in those days. All glory to the Lamb who is worthy.

The Gandhidham church grew steadily, and the Lord opened doors for several baptismal services there. The church’s minister and secretary at the time was K. V. Abraham, now the IPCNR Secretary, and the treasurer was K. M. Johny, who served in the IPCNR for many years before moving to the United States. The Lord was expanding the work on every side.

The Family Comes to Jamnagar

Toward the end of July, Thomas wrote to Marykutty and the children asking them to come straight to Jamnagar from Kerala, letting her know he would meet them in Mumbai when they arrived. The week before the family was due to leave Kerala, he was ministering in Gandhidham. After the service, he flew to Jamnagar and met Mathews at the airstrip adjacent to the Air Force camp.

His first question was direct: “Brother, is there any mail for me? A money order?” A letter had come, but no money order. His heart sank. He needed to leave for Mumbai the next morning, or Marykutty, arriving in a city where she knew no one and had four children in tow, would find no one waiting for her. He had no money.

He had believed a money order would come. He had said so with certainty, even while there was nothing to show for it. Mathews was distressed too. Then someone thought to call a Malayali brother who worked at the main post office in Jamnagar city, a Mar Thomite. Mathews reached him by phone and asked whether any money order had arrived for Pastor K. T. Thomas. “Nothing so far,” the man said, “but the evening mail bag has just come in. I’m not sure if there’s anything in it.” At Mathews’s urging, he agreed to check.

They waited. Then the answer came back: there was a money order, two hundred rupees, sent by Pastor P. M. Samuel. Thomas collected it that same day and praised God with everything he had. For Mathews, it was a moment that lodged itself deep in his faith. He had watched Thomas declare with calm certainty that the money order would be there, while there was nothing visible to support the claim. Hebrews 11:1 had just walked off the page and into real life.

How faithful is the God who works wonders precisely when the need is greatest.

Thomas left for Mumbai the next morning. Marykutty and the children arrived safely, just as planned. The family praised God together and shared what He had been doing through their various new situations and places. They made it to Jamnagar without incident and settled into Sajjan Nivas, abode of the righteous, to carry on the Lord’s work.

Thankamma Goes to America

Around this time, the first member of the Delhi congregation left for the United States. Thankamma, the younger sister of Rosamma who had served alongside Pastor P. M. Samuel, had been a genuine blessing to the Delhi church. She was gifted in various ways and brought a real brightness to the congregation.

Thomas and his family had first met her during their Motibagh days, and from that very first meeting she had prayed over Thomas and spoken with striking accuracy about what the Spirit was revealing to her: his calling, the grace God was giving him for ministry, and the expanding work He intended to do through him. Believers like her, selfless and truly devoted, have always been an ornament to the church of God.

Ahmedabad and Maninagar

The meetings continued to bear fruit wherever Thomas went. In 1965, he held meetings in Ahmedabad, and the services at Maninagar alongside Dr. Raj and his family were especially rich. What had been planned as one week was extended to two because of what the Spirit was clearly doing.

Faridabad, Ghaziabad, and Kanpur

Brothers from the Delhi congregation had been holding meetings in Faridabad and Ghaziabad, and Thomas would join them periodically to support and encourage the work. That same year, God opened a door in Kanpur as well. Jacob Thomas, an Air Force personnel who had transferred there from Delhi, first extended the invitation, and Phillipose and his family became key partners in the work. The Delhi convention that year was also fruitful; among those baptized was K. C. Samuel, now the IPCNR Treasurer.

Pastor P. S. Thomas Comes to Gandhidham

In 1965, Thomas brought Pastor P. S. Thomas from Kerala to serve the Jamnagar congregation. Since Gandhidham had more believers at the time than Jamnagar, however, he sent Pastor P. S. Thomas and his family there instead. That servant of God gave many years of commendable service in northern India before eventually settling in Houston, Texas. Thomas also attended the Itarsi Convention that year, a gathering led primarily by Pastor Kurien Thomas.

The Work Begins in Baroda

On the way back from the Itarsi Convention, a brother from Thomas’s home region had arranged a four-day gospel series at a Methodist church in Baroda. When the notice was printed, it listed only “Mr. K. T. Thomas” as the speaker. The organizer had done this deliberately: he knew that if people found out the preacher was a Pentecostal pastor, the denominational Christians in town would stay away.

Thomas brought Mathew Cherian and his family with him from Itarsi in central India. The organizer had given one clear instruction in advance: preach the gospel, but say nothing about baptism. Thomas has said since that he has never understood what a gospel without baptism would look like, since baptism is woven into the gospel itself (Acts 8:35-38). Out of respect for the organizer’s good intentions, he held back for two days. On the third day, the Spirit took over and he preached on baptism with full conviction. By God’s grace, no one took offense; and afterward, two people came forward asking to be baptized, one of them the very brother who had asked Thomas to leave the subject alone.

The Sunday morning before the baptismal service, Thomas gave a thorough Bible study on baptism: its importance, who should be baptized, what it means, and why infant sprinkling falls short of what Scripture intends. When he asked, moved by the Spirit, who was ready to obey this precious command of the Lord, four more people stood up in tears and presented themselves for baptism. Six were baptized in the water that day, bearing witness to Jesus Christ. The brothers quickly arranged dry clothes for them. Everyone present felt the nearness of heaven. Afterward, Mrs. Mathew Cherian said quietly, “If the Word allowed a second baptism, I would step in right now.” That was the measure of blessing on the service.

Mathew Cherian in Baroda

Thomas told the newly baptized believers that Mathew Cherian would stay and minister to them while he came back periodically to support the work. A house near Bajwa was rented for sixty rupees a month. After settling Mathew Cherian and his family there and leaving them what money he had, Thomas returned to Jamnagar.

For the next two years he made regular trips to Baroda to minister. Mathew Cherian eventually joined a fellowship based in Itarsi.

Some years later, a dispute arose in the Baroda congregation and a group of brothers decided to meet separately for worship. They contacted Thomas, making clear that if the IPC Northern Region would not recognize them, they would join another fellowship. Thomas went, held several days of meetings, and took the work under his care. In 1973 he sent Pastor K. Joy to serve as their minister. Mathew Cherian continued working there for several more years and now lives in the United States. The fellowship’s work in Baroda goes on to this day. That is how Pentecostal witness began in that city. Thomas also held meetings in Rajkot and Ahmedabad during these years.

Hardship in Jamnagar

Through the Jamnagar years, Thomas and his family passed through real and sustained hardship. He has said that for Marykutty, Jamnagar was a school of faith, a place where she learned to trust God more deeply and discovered firsthand the glory of a life lived in total dependence on Him. Marykutty has described two incidents from those years in her own words:

Flour And Oil

“Pastor Thomas was away in gospel work, and the children and I were going through an extremely difficult stretch. Every rupee was gone. There was not a trace of flour or oil left in the house. We ate what little food remained for our midday meal, and afterward I prayed with a heavy heart: ‘Lord, Your servant is not here. What am I to give the children this evening? There is not even a little flour or oil.’ And then, for the first time in my life, I heard a clear voice from God. I had known the Lord speak through Scripture as I read and meditated on it, but hearing Him directly like this was something entirely new.

“Daughter, why are you so troubled about your needs? I have already made provision for you.” I looked around. No one was there.

“Lord, is that You?” I asked again. I reasoned in my heart: no one knows there is no flour or oil in this house. I have not told a soul. How could anyone possibly know and bring it here? As I prayed and turned all of this over, I drifted into a light sleep. And in that moment, I seemed to see old Uncle Matthaichan from the congregation standing before me.

“This was around two in the afternoon. Time moved slowly.

“At six in the evening, Uncle Matthaichan knocked at the door. When I opened it, he was standing there with fifteen kilograms of wheat flour in his right hand and a bottle of oil in his left. I broke into tears. I asked him who had told him we were out of flour and oil. His answer left me speechless.

‘At two o’clock, after my lunch break at the Woolen Mills, I was back at work when a deep unrest came over me. It was as though the Lord was stirring my heart, saying: “My servant’s household has no flour or oil. Go and bring it to them quickly.” When I finished my shift and got home, I noticed our own supply was running low as well, so I bought wheat, had it ground, picked up oil, and came straight here.’

We told each other what had happened and praised God together. I had come from a Brethren Assembly background, and God was teaching me, step by step, what it means to walk by faith. How good God is.”

Rent and School Fees

“There was another time when Pastor Thomas was away in ministry and we could not pay two months of rent. School fees for the children, everyday necessities, everything was stretched beyond its limit. I decided to fast and pray for four days. I wrote out a list of needs: two hundred rupees for two months of rent, the children’s school fees, other essential expenses, and twenty rupees to send to a servant of God who was struggling in ministry back home in Kerala. The total came to four hundred and twenty rupees. I opened my Bible to Matthew 7, to the passage that begins ‘Ask, and it will be given to you,’ placed the list inside, and began to pray.

“On the first day of the fast, a letter arrived from one K. M. Thomas in Delhi, someone who had never met us. He had heard while in Delhi that we had left secure employment to serve the Lord in Jamnagar by faith. He and another brother had been praying one night when he heard what he described as a voice from God: ‘My servant K. T. Thomas’s family is in hardship.’ He had struggled to find our address but somehow came across a notice from a convention we had held in Jamnagar. He wrote to say he wanted to help and asked us to write back confirming the address. I forwarded his letter to my husband.

“When Thomas later visited Delhi, he went to meet this brother. In their conversation, Thomas said, ‘I did not come to ask you for anything. I came to marvel with you at how God works. You were in one place, my wife and children were in another, and I was in a third. And yet the God who knows all things knew exactly where each of us was and what we needed.’ K. M. Thomas is now, we understand, in Abu Dhabi.

“I kept fasting. On the second day, I heard a voice again: ‘Daughter, I have heard your prayer. Stop the fast. There is no need to keep praying for these things. I have already made provision.’

“I was cautious. Was this a scheme of the enemy to cut the fast short? I kept praying, and to settle it the voice came once more: ‘Today you will receive seventy-five rupees. The full four hundred and twenty rupees I will provide in various ways.’

“I trusted God and waited. Shortly afterward, the postman came with a money order for exactly seventy-five rupees, sent by Pastor P. M. Samuel in Andhra. It was the first money order I had personally signed for since we stepped out in faith. That same day I sent twenty rupees of it to the servant of God struggling in Kerala. Within ten days, to the exact rupee, the full four hundred and twenty arrived, just as God had said. How good God is.”

The Baptism of the Holy Spirit in Jamnagar

Thomas testifies to an event in early January 1965 that he says he will never forget as long as he lives. It began when he traveled to Gandhidham in mid-December 1964 and held a series of special prayer meetings, fasting sessions, waiting meetings, and Bible classes. The New Year’s Eve watch-night service was a deep blessing. Through those days, the Lord brought several people to salvation and into the anointing of the Spirit.

Afterward, Thomas returned to Jamnagar. The very day he arrived, the brothers came to see him at the Faith Home. In his absence, John, a member of one Brethren Assembly, had kept the meetings going. At that point, Thomas was the only person in Jamnagar who had been baptized in the Holy Spirit. Even Marykutty, coming from a Brethren Assembly background, had not yet received this experience.

That same evening Thomas told Marykutty: “I’m setting aside a few days for fasting and prayer. Every believer here needs to receive the anointing of the Spirit.” When John came to visit later that evening and Thomas shared the same, John’s response was blunt: “Brother, you’re already exhausted. Why put yourself through another fast?”

The next morning the fast began. That evening’s meeting was held at the Faith Home. John, who had never once missed a meeting, did not come. Everyone else was there. Thomas taught on the baptism of the Holy Spirit, a subject he had never addressed in Jamnagar before. The chief reason he had held back was that he knew John was opposed to it. He invited everyone present to seek this experience and closed the meeting in prayer.

The following evening, the meeting was at John’s house. On the way over, Thomas said to Marykutty, “What am I going to preach tonight? If I bring up the baptism of the Holy Spirit again, it will put John off.”

Marykutty’s answer: “Pray, and preach whatever God puts on your heart.”

They stopped first at the home of Kuriachan, the Pentecostal believer who lived next door to John. When they knocked, Kuriachan ran to the door and immediately motioned them to be quiet. It soon became clear why. The previous night, Kuriachan’s wife and Mrs. John had been discussing the teaching on the Holy Spirit baptism, and John had overheard. He had not taken it well. When someone mentioned that Thomas had been teaching on the subject, his reaction was: “Has that demon found its way here too?”

Kuriachan told Thomas that John was in a very unsettled state. Thomas took it calmly and walked over. John looked drawn and pale.

The evening service began. About eighteen people had gathered in his home. After singing and prayer, Thomas picked up where he had left off the previous evening and preached again on the baptism of the Holy Spirit. He invited everyone to pray and seek this experience.

Marykutty prayed first: “Lord, grant this precious experience to me and to John.”

To everyone’s surprise, John began to pray as well. After only two sentences, something broke open in the room. People spontaneously began to praise God and cry out “Hallelujah!” Some were filled with the Spirit. The sound grew, and John stopped praying, stepped out to the veranda, and paced back and forth. Neighbors heard the noise and began gathering outside to see what was happening. Inside, Marykutty and several others had already received the anointing, speaking in tongues and prophesying.

Thomas glanced at his watch. It was nine o’clock. The last bus had left at eight-thirty. Just as he was about to bring the meeting to a close, Mrs. John came running in from the veranda, handed her small baby to her husband who was still standing outside, and dropped to her knees. Thomas asked what she needed.

“Pastor,” she said, “the joy inside me is overwhelming.”

Thomas raised his hand and said, “Receive the baptism of the Holy Spirit in the name of Jesus.”

Before the words had fully left his mouth, she broke out in tongues at full volume and began to dance in the Spirit.

Many who were there that night received the anointing. John himself, in the days that followed, also came under the power of the Spirit. Thomas carries the memory of that night as one of the most unforgettable blessings of his entire life. To God be the glory.

The 1965 Indo-Pak War: Air Raids in Jamnagar

While Pastor K. V. Kuriachan was serving the Chembur congregation in Mumbai, Thomas traveled there for a four-day series of meetings. The first evening, the service was held in a brother’s home. Midway through the message, the electricity went out. Over the radio came the news: Pakistan had attacked India. Pakistani bombers were heading for Mumbai. The entire city had been placed under a blackout. The brothers also heard Radio Pakistan reporting that Pakistani aircraft had struck Jamnagar. Thomas heard none of this. He had no idea.

The meeting ended abruptly. Pastor K. V. Kuriachan turned to Thomas and said, “Given the situation, I don’t think we can continue the meetings. Let’s plan for another time.” The next morning, Thomas set out for Jamnagar. At the railway station, he picked up a newspaper. Only then did he read that Jamnagar had been bombed.

What the brothers in Mumbai had known the previous night, they had kept from him on purpose. Because his family was in Jamnagar, they had quietly arranged for the meetings to be cancelled and sent him on his way, knowing that telling him the truth would only add to his anguish without helping anyone.

He read the reports. Jamnagar had been badly hit. People had fled. His mind went immediately to Marykutty and the children. “Lord,” he prayed as he traveled, “You placed us in Jamnagar for Your work. But now, where is my family? Where do I even begin to look?”

He boarded the train at Viramgam station, heading toward Jamnagar on the route toward Okha. The train reached Rajkot. What he saw there stopped him cold. The station and its surroundings were packed with panicked people pressing in from every direction. Residents of Jamnagar and the surrounding areas had poured into Rajkot by bullock cart and whatever else they could find, fifty miles of desperate flight from the bombing. Thomas’s dread for his family deepened with every moment.

Then came another blow. An announcement over the station’s public address system: the train to Jamnagar had been cancelled. The tracks had been damaged. He made his way to the restroom at the end of the carriage, sat down, and wept. And prayed. “Lord, please let this train go at least as far as Jamnagar.” He waited. Three and a half hours passed.

Then another announcement: the train that had been scheduled for Okha would proceed only as far as Jamnagar. Nothing beyond. Thomas praised God and climbed back aboard.

The train crept slowly forward. In his own words:

“The train finally made it to Jamnagar. The station was deserted. The streets around it were utterly silent, like a graveyard. Not a person in sight. ‘Lord,’ I cried, ‘What do I do? Where do I go? Where do I look for my family?’ I began walking toward the house, praying every step of the way.

“About a kilometer and a half later, I came around to the back of the building. And there, to my astonishment, was my family, safe and completely unharmed. In that part of the city, nearly everyone had fled. Only my family and one Hindi-speaking Brahmin family who had stayed to help them remained. We praised God with everything we had. How do you put into words the mercy of the Lord who kept our family through those terrifying days without a single harm? God is good. God is faithful. Hallelujah.”

The Brahmin neighbor came over afterward and told Thomas: “I knew that because you love and serve God, He would not allow any disaster to come to you. That is why, even when everyone else ran, we stayed with your family.”

The threat of bombing had been circulating for some time before it materialized. At Rejimon’s school, trenches had been dug all around the grounds, and the city authorities had trained both children and adults to drop into them and lie flat when the bombers came. Rejimon had told his mother all of this. The night after Thomas left for Mumbai, the bombing began.

Marykutty gathered the four children around her, two on each side, and prayed with a breaking heart: “Lord, Your servant is not here. If he were, it would at least be a comfort. In his absence, be our help, mine and the children’s. There is no one else we can lean on.”

Almost immediately, she heard a voice: “Daughter, your husband is only a man. I will watch over you the way a mother watches over her children.” Those words settled something in her. She comforted the children, and they endured the night together.

Most of Jamnagar’s residents had fled to Rajkot by nightfall. In that stretch of the neighborhood, only Marykutty and the children and the Hindu Brahmin family next door remained. From the trenches they could see Pakistani bombers flying low, dropping their bombs, fire scattering in every direction. Two L-shaped trenches sheltered both families through the nights.

Two brothers from the Air Force were also in a trench that night, along with three friends. The bombing killed all three on one side of the trench. The Lord preserved His own on the other. As for the city itself, the damage was less than feared; most of the bombs had missed their targets, falling outside both the city and the Air Force camp.

Thomas and his family spent the next eighteen nights sleeping outside, in the trenches. The bombers came each evening at dusk. After Thomas returned from Mumbai, three more bombing raids followed. They were nights of discovering, firsthand, the goodness of God. The believing brothers from the Air Force camp came through regularly during those dark days to check on them and bring what comfort they could, and Thomas and his family remember it with gratitude to this day.

While the people of Jamnagar spent those nights paralyzed with fear, counting each moment in dread, this family found shelter hidden in the Rock of Ages, and rested there without fear.

The war forced the brothers in the Air Force station to send their families back to Kerala. Other Christians fled to various places. As a result, the congregation dwindled sharply. Thomas wrote to Delhi explaining the circumstances. The brothers there urged him to return at once. Thomas and his family packed up and headed back to Delhi.

Back in Delhi

When Thomas and his family left for Jamnagar, they had already vacated the house Sister Nathaniel had provided in South Extension. The congregation had since rented a place in Raj Nagar for worship and other gatherings, and on the occasions Thomas came to Delhi once every three months, that was where he stayed. Shortly before the family returned to Delhi from Jamnagar, the brothers moved again, this time to a house in Lajpat Nagar III. That became home and the center of ministry for the next season, even as Thomas continued making periodic trips back to Jamnagar.

An Anglo-Indian Woman Is Delivered

During their time at Lajpat Nagar, Thomas set aside several days for fasting and prayer. One evening, five people were praying together: Thomas, Marykutty, Mrs. K. D. Timothy, Sister Ammini,1 and an Anglo-Indian woman from the congregation.

While they were praying, an evil spirit in the Anglo-Indian woman manifested itself and caused a serious disturbance. Thomas rebuked it in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the woman was quieted and set free.

An American Missionary Team

While the family was still at Lajpat Nagar III, a missionary from America arrived in Delhi with a team and spent a week conducting meetings organized by Thomas and the congregation. The services were richly blessed. People came to faith, the sick were healed, and the name of God was glorified.

The missionary made every effort to bring Thomas under the umbrella of his organization. He laid out an ambitious vision: Thomas would oversee the entire India operation, with others serving under him, and the scope would include a Bible school, an orphanage, and other large-scale programs. He showed Thomas detailed blueprints of the whole plan.

Thomas’s answer was clear. “We are doing the Lord’s work freely, and we have no desire to do it under another organization’s label. If you want to support the work we are already doing, we would gladly receive that help and carry on. But joining another movement is not something we can do.”

The missionary was genuinely disappointed, but they parted on respectful terms, agreeing that Thomas would give a final answer when the team returned to Delhi after holding meetings in other cities. When they came back a month later and held a few more days of meetings, Thomas had by then moved from the cramped first house to a better-equipped one at National Park nearby, the Lord having opened that door. Seeing this, the missionary said, “You really are a man of faith.” But that didn’t change Thomas’s decision; they parted ways.

John Daniel

John Daniel, a pastor in New York city, began his ministry in Delhi. The year was 1966. He arrived in Delhi after completing two years of study at the Doon Bible College. John lived with the Thomas family in Lajpat Nagar for about a year, taking an active part in the ministry. In 1967, Thomas sent him to serve in Guwahati, Assam. Much later, he moved to New York city.

Timothy and the Jamnagar Church

When Timothy resigned from his job to pursue full-time gospel work, Thomas sent him to Jamnagar to give the work there a boost. Timothy found his footing quickly and asked to stay on as the congregation’s minister, so Thomas appointed him. In time, Timothy and the congregation affiliated with the Fellowship Church. That arrangement eventually ran into serious trouble, and most of the believers walked away. Those who remained were unanimous in wanting to come under the IPC Northern Region, which formally recognized the work in 1973. The following year, the author of this biography was sent to serve as their minister. Some years later, Pastor Timothy sold a property the church had purchased and returned to Kerala. He has since, by all accounts, rejoined the IPC and is active in ministry there.

Meanwhile, the work across the Delhi area kept growing by God’s grace. That year’s convention brought Pastors P. M. Samuel and Kurien Thomas to preach, and through their ministry souls were won and the name of Jesus was lifted high.

The Language of Separation

Pastor M. K. Chacko had long been hoping to bring his Hindi congregation and Thomas’s Malayalam congregation together into a single fellowship. Thomas put it to his people, and most were against it. He held out for a while, but eventually gave in to Pastor Chacko’s persistence. The two congregations merged in early 1967. It didn’t hold. The vision each man carried for his ministry was simply too different, and within six months they were meeting separately again. The friendship between them, however, remained as warm as ever.

During the merger, Thomas had moved from National Park to M-18 in Green Park Extension, closer to Pastor Chacko’s assembly hall, to make working together more practical. When the arrangement fell apart, he was left with a problem: dropping the Malayalam service had driven away a good number of Malayali members, and winning them back took real effort. Then the landlord objected to services being held in the house. And the rent had become impossible to manage. The house had to go.

That was when Brother P. N. Kurien, Director of All India Prayer Fellowship, stepped in and offered his own home at M-14, Green Park Extension, free of charge, for Thomas and his family to live in and hold worship services.

Aleykutty Believes

The M-14 season was one of genuine financial hardship. Staying with the family at the time was Aleykutty, a relative of Marykutty’s and a firm member of the Jacobite tradition. The singing, the prayer, the whole texture of how this family lived by faith—none of it meant anything to her. She watched it all from a distance, unmoved and uninterested.

Then came a day when there was nothing to eat in the house. Thomas was away in ministry. Marykutty did not want Aleykutty to see the state they were in, so she slipped into a room, closed the door, wrote out everything the family needed on a piece of paper, and brought it before God in tears.

A knock at the door. The postman. Aleykutty went to answer it and came to find Marykutty. There was a money order. When Marykutty looked at the amount, it covered every item on the list she had been weeping over just moments before.

Aleykutty had seen the whole thing. She could not explain it away. The God this family trusted had shown up with exact provision at the precise moment of need, and that was enough. She came to faith, was baptized, and joined the fellowship. She and her family now live in Ernakulam.

That same year, T. G. Oommen2 was brought from Kerala to teach a month-long Bible class in Delhi. It is worth noting that those classes were especially fruitful and richly blessed.

Key Co-Workers

It would be a particular joy to name some of the brothers who played a central role in Thomas’s ministry during this period. Brothers Titus, Johny Ninan, and T. K. George were among the most significant. Brother T. K. George served faithfully alongside the congregation for many years in an especially blessed way, and now lives with his family in Syracuse, New York, continuing in the Lord’s work.

The selfless service of these brothers deserves to be remembered. Many souls were brought to the Lord through their efforts, and that is something of enduring worth. The Lord has blessed each one in their sphere and continues to use them wherever He has placed them. To His name be the praise.

Thomas has shared in his own words what the Lord told him when he first received the call to ministry:

“I will give you young brothers as helpers. You must train them to be useful in My work. But I will not let them stay with you for long; I will need to send them to various places so they can be of greater use.”

That word proved true, again and again, in the lives of many who served alongside him over the years.

The convention that year was blessed, with Pastors P. M. Samuel and P. M. Philip ministering the Word.

From M-14 to V-10

Word reached Thomas that the property at V-10, just nearby, might be available to rent. He went to see it and liked what he found. The family needed a proper home, and the need had become urgent, so he began fasting and praying about it.

The owner was out of town when Thomas came by, but when he heard about the visit, he made his way to M-14 to find Thomas. It happened to be a Sunday, and a service was in progress. After it ended, he invited Thomas to come take a proper look at V-10. Thomas went, brought a few of the brothers along, and the house was everything they needed. The asking rent was 750 rupees a month.

Thomas didn’t hedge. “I’m a gospel worker,” he said. “The church gives me roughly five hundred rupees a month. I’m willing to hand over every rupee of it without keeping anything back for myself, but I cannot go higher than that.”

The owner listened. As Thomas and the brothers turned to leave, he called them back.

“I am a police inspector,” he said. “I have never earned so much as a dishonest paisa in my life. I am a man who fears God. Because you are serving God, you can have the house for the five hundred.”

They stood there barely able to take it in. How good God is. Thomas and his family moved into V-10 on December 30, 1967, just in time to hold the year-end crossing-over service there.

The Stronghold at V-10 Crumbles

The trouble started that very first afternoon. The youngest child woke from a nap terrified, screaming that there were figures standing in the room. By two o’clock the child was inconsolable, and from that point the presence of an evil spirit made itself felt with increasing force. That night, people who had lain down to sleep were thrown from their beds onto the floor. Cries broke out in the darkness. The whole household was in turmoil.

Thomas cried out to God and went to prayer. He followed that with fasting, pressing in for deliverance and rebuking the enemy with authority. The battle was real, but it did not last. The enemy was broken. The Lord’s name was glorified even there.

Thomas and his family would live at V-10 for nine years, ministering from that house for the entirety of that season.

Not long after they moved in, the police inspector received a promotion to Deputy Superintendent of Police. One morning he arrived at V-10 with his wife, overcome with emotion. He fell at Thomas’s feet and said through tears, “Your God has blessed me. For years I was passed over and I was going through a very difficult time. But from the moment I gave this house to you for your home and your worship, your God stepped in for me, and that is why this promotion came the way it did.”

Thomas lifted him to his feet and told him about the Lord. He would be promoted again in the years that followed, eventually retiring as a Deputy Commissioner. He has said since that it was through these experiences that the fear of God took hold of him, and that was why he had kept the rent so low for all those years.

Bethel Bible Institute

The year 1968 stands out for many reasons. Pastor K. E. Abraham, founder of the Indian Pentecostal Church (IPC), visited Delhi along with Pastor George Varghese and stayed with Thomas and his family at V-10. It was also the year one of Thomas’s deepest desires finally came to fruition: to run a Bible school funded by Indians, training at least ten workers to send into the field. On March 24, 1968, Pastor K. E. Abraham officially inaugurated Bethel Bible School. The convention that same year was a blessing, with Pastors K. E. Abraham and George Varghese ministering the Word.

Fifteen students enrolled that first year. The teaching faculty included Pastors T. S. Abraham, George Philip, T. J. Simon, and K. T. Thomas, along with Brothers P. G. Abraham, P. T. Mathew, E. V. Joseph, and P. M. Varghese, and Sister Leyyamma.

A camp held with the Bible school students in Modi Nagar, Uttar Pradesh, led to the planting of a new congregation there as well.

The brothers who studied in 1968 are serving the Lord in various places today. John Skariah went to serve in Faridabad. K. Joy served first alongside Thomas in Delhi, then moved to Ambala. Through those years, the work kept spreading. New congregations were planted in Bareilly in Uttar Pradesh, in Ludhiana, Adampur in Punjab, in Jammu in Jammu and Kashmir, and in Siliguri in Bengal. Within Delhi, work also began in Punjabi Bagh and Maurice Nagar.

In Adampur and Hoshiarpur, T. M. Philip and G. Chacko worked together. T. M. Philip has since gone to be with the Lord. G. Chacko eventually returned to Kerala and later moved to the United States, where he now lives with his family in Philadelphia, serving the Lord while working there.

Around this time, the Lord also opened a door for work to begin in Gurugram, Haryana.

Fifty Days of Fasting

The year 1969 witnessed several events that would prove unforgettable. From November 16, 1968 through January 4, 1969, Thomas set aside fifty days for fasting and prayer. They were days marked by great blessing from the Lord. Some people received the anointing of the Spirit during that time, sick people were healed, and the name of God was glorified.

The Korba Convention

The Second Regional Convention

The second annual convention of the Northern Region was held in Delhi from November 3 to 7, 1971. A refresher course for ministers was organized the week before, beginning October 26. The late Pastors P. M. Samuel and T. G. Oommen were the speakers, with Pastor T. G. Oommen handling most of the Bible classes. The recently departed Pastor K. K. The First American Tour

On June 28, 1972, K. T. Thomas set foot in the United States for the first time. He traveled to New York, Washington D.C., Virginia, Alabama, Houston, Dallas, Florida, Chicago, Philadelphia, Indiana, Detroit, Kansas, and several other cities, holding meetings throughout. He also visited Toronto and Windsor in Canada.

The Indian presence in America at that time was a fraction of what it is today, and most of Thomas’s ministry was among Americans. At one small independent church, he held Bible classes and meetings continuously for three weeks. Many came to faith and were baptized. When he left, the church gave him fifty dollars. He mentions it in passing, with characteristic understatement; material gain had never been what drove him. The point of the journey was the ministry itself, and beyond that, the joy of being reunited with brothers and sisters from the Delhi congregation who had scattered to various parts of the world. He speaks of their sincere and selfless fellowship with deep gratitude to this day.

The Toronto Reunion

During his Canadian visit, Thomas attended a Sunday service in Toronto where brothers and sisters who had come from Delhi gathered to worship together. Old memories surfaced, old bonds were renewed, and songs that Thomas himself had written and led over the years were sung again with feeling. A similar gathering took place in New York, where former Delhi congregation members came together to remember God’s grace and the paths He had walked them through.

Singapore

Not long after returning to Delhi, Thomas left again, this time for Singapore, to attend a leadership training program at the Haggai Institute. While there, he preached in several places and saw ten people come to faith. Six were baptized first; the remaining four followed. The photographs are included in this book. Wherever he went, the hunger for souls went with him.

The Third Regional Convention

Back in Delhi, Thomas then traveled to Patna, the capital of Bihar, for that year’s regional convention, held November 8 to 12. Pastor C. D. Samuel and the Patna congregation handled all the arrangements. It was the first convention of the Northern Region held outside Delhi. The late Pastor P. M. Philip preached. Evangelists K. Joy and E. M. Thomas interpreted into Hindi.

God’s Ways

Thomas has spoken about something that happened in 1972 that stayed with him. In the early days of the Northern Region, he and his family were under considerable financial strain. Two ministers wrote to him saying they were struggling to pay their rent and had nowhere to turn. The letter sat heavily on him. He prayed, asking God to open a way to help them.

On the third day, a letter arrived in the post. It was from abroad. He opened it. Inside was a check for two hundred dollars. No letter. No name. The postmark was five days old, which was unusually fast for international mail to reach India. To this day, no one knows who sent it.

He cashed the check and used it to help the two ministers and cover the household’s immediate needs.

He shared this at the 1972 Patna convention as a testimony, glorifying God for providing through an anonymous hand at exactly the right moment. A prominent IPC leader who was present leaned over and said: “Thomachan, don’t go telling people when you receive money. They’ll stop giving.”

Marykutty, who heard this, replied without hesitation. “Must we talk about everything else God does and hide the part about the money? Is that giving Him the glory? This needs to be told.”

1973: The Work Spreads

In 1973, the Lord opened a new work in Shahdara, Delhi, and in several new locations in other states. New ministers also joined the region. The IPCNR congregations in and around Delhi held combined worship services.

In the second week of April, Thomas traveled to Pune and Mumbai for ministry. The Regional Council meeting was held in Mumbai on April 19, 1973.

In May, Thomas sent his family to Kerala, then went to Calcutta, followed by Baroda, for meetings. The Lord opened a new work in Calcutta through the Northern Region, with Pastor C. T. Thomas and Brother John Thomas playing a central role. In early June, Thomas went home to Kerala and brought the whole family back to Delhi.

Back to America

In late June, Thomas made his second visit to the United States, holding meetings in New York, Washington, Texas, and several other places. He also visited Canada again and bore witness to the Almighty there too. He returned to Delhi at the end of October.

While he was in America, the Lord gave him and Marykutty another daughter. On September 7, 1973, Jiji (officially, Persis Thomas1) was born. With five children now, the Almighty had blessed them richly.

The Youngest Son’s Illness

During his third visit to the United States in 1975, Thomas was away on the other side of the globe when news came that his youngest son, Joji, had been struck by typhoid and was critically ill.

For thirty days, the family had managed at home, Joji refusing to go to the hospital. He kept saying the same thing: “Pray, just pray; don’t take me to the hospital unless I lose consciousness.” Then he lost consciousness. The brothers and sisters rushed him in.

The doctors refused to take the case. There was no hope, they said. His blood was fully infected. He would not survive forty-eight hours. It was only at the insistence of the believing nurses among them that he was admitted at all, and even then, no treatment was given.2

Seven days passed. Joji lay unconscious, unmoving. The doctors came twice a day to see the other patient in the room and would glance briefly in Joji’s direction before leaving. There was nothing to be done, and they all knew it.

Marykutty sat through those days watching it happen. Every morning and evening, the doctors came, tended to the other patient, looked once at Joji, and walked out. Her heart was breaking. She wept and prayed, pouring out her grief before the Lord, asking for her son’s life.

On the seventh day, Joji opened his eyes.

The doctors said so themselves: it was something medicine could not explain. Slowly, he recovered and came home. Through every one of those seven days, the congregation had been fasting and praying. Thomas was half a world away, praying with the believers there. The God who hears prayer worked a miracle. To Him be the glory!

Moving to F-58, Green Park

The year 1976 brought grief and joy in roughly equal measure. Early that year, the owner of V-10 asked Thomas to vacate. The cause was the enforcement of a law during the Emergency, requiring government employees who owned private property to give up their government-allotted quarters. Thomas negotiated a little time, agreeing to leave when the schools broke for summer in May.

Thomas and the congregation searched the surrounding area for a suitable house. Nothing came through. On March 30, 1976, this author arrived in Delhi from Jamnagar to serve as associate minister of the Green Park congregation. He lived in the ESI Colony and ministered from there while also helping at Green Park, since no house had yet been found.

When the school holidays arrived in April and the deadline to vacate could no longer be postponed, Thomas took his family to Kerala. While they were there, word came that a three-story house at F-58 was for sale. The price was three hundred and fifty thousand rupees, with the full amount to be paid within three months.

Brother Varghese C. John, the Green Park congregation’s secretary at the time, sent a telegram to Thomas in Kerala. Thomas came back to Delhi immediately, saw the house, and wanted it. The congregation had only twenty thousand rupees in its building fund. But Thomas signed an agreement: twenty thousand as a down payment, with the full amount plus interest to be paid within two years. They moved in and began holding services.

The money was slow to come. Months passed. The situation tightened: if the full amount was not paid, everything already deposited would be forfeited. Thomas looked for others who might purchase the upper floor. No one came forward. At that point the congregation’s leaders pressed Thomas to buy it himself.

He refused.

“If I get personally involved in this,” he said, “it will cast a shadow over everything I have stood for until now. Our people won’t let it go. Even if we lose every rupee we’ve put in, I will never purchase this property.”

The congregation’s secretary at the time, Brother V. P. Thomas, pushed harder.

“You’ll have to sell your house and land back home and find a way to make this work. There’s no other option.”

With no other path open, Thomas finally gave in. He sold the property he owned in Kerala at a significant loss. What that brought in wasn’t enough. He took a bank loan to cover the rest, and in August 1980, four years after signing the original agreement, the purchase was completed.

The congregation paid two hundred thousand rupees for the ground floor, and Thomas paid two hundred thousand for the upper portion. It should be said plainly: the congregation’s share had largely been raised through Thomas’s own effort, traveling through India, the United States, and the Gulf, gathering contributions from believers and churches wherever he went. The ownership was later documented in writing in the proportion of the contributions: 2:2.5 in favor of the congregation.

When the bank loan was taken, a complication arose: banks would lend only to individuals, not to churches. The Green Park congregation council therefore authorized Thomas to purchase the building in his own name. Those documents are on file with the congregation. That is how F-58 came to be registered under Pastor K. T. Thomas’s name in 1980. The building has since served the many ministries of the IPC Northern Region: the Bible school, the residential staff, the regional office, and more. The sacrifice behind that building is something that cannot be easily measured.

That same year, Thomas made his fourth visit to the United States, leaving July 3, 1976 and returning to Delhi on December 1.

Pastor M. K. Chacko’s Heart Attack

On Sunday morning, February 6, 1977, Pastor M. K. Chacko suffered a severe heart attack. The call came to Thomas early. He was still dressing when the phone rang again. The pastor’s condition was worse. He was asked to go immediately. He took a taxi to Chacko’s home in East of Kailash.

The moment Thomas saw him, he knew it was serious. He suggested taking him to the hospital right then, in the taxi that was waiting. Chacko raised his hand: no. He beckoned Thomas to come close, took Thomas’s hand, placed it on his own chest, and asked him to pray.

Thomas knelt and prayed for a long time, his heart breaking. “Lord, don’t take Your servant now. He has labored so much for You.”

He stayed at Chacko’s side, not leaving. Someone offered a small pill. Chacko refused it. This man of faith had never taken medicine in his life, and he was not about to start at the end of it.

Thomas arranged for the author of this biography to lead the Green Park service that Sunday morning, and stayed with Chackochan through the day. That evening, Brother Samuel Cheriyan, son of the late Pastor K. C. Cheriyan, also arrived and helped through the night. On Monday morning, February 7, Pastor Chacko’s eldest son M. C. Abraham arrived from Bhopal, and only then did Thomas finally go home. By that point, Chacko had stabilized considerably.

In 1977, the Lord opened several new ministry stations, and Pastor K. V. Philip along with his congregation joined the Northern Region.

The Gathering Storm

1982 was the hardest year of K. T. Thomas’s life. It was the year he lost a son.

The Accident

On January 18, Thomas left for Kerala to attend the Kumbanad Convention. After the convention and General Council meetings on January 24, he had planned to travel on to Poonaloor. But something stopped him. A quiet, insistent prompting turned him toward Thiruvananthapuram instead. He boarded the Venad Express at Thiruvalla railway station that evening, not knowing why.

He was at his cousin’s home in Thiruvananthapuram when the phone rang. It was there that he first heard about the accident. If he had gone to Poonaloor as planned, the news would have reached him much later, and he would have lost hours he did not have. Through the night, the calls kept coming. By the time one of them brought the news of Saji’s death, Thomas was on his knees, spending the dark hours in prayer. In the morning he went to Poonaloor, told the family, and returned to Thiruvananthapuram. On Monday, January 25, he flew to Delhi with his cousin.

Here is what had happened.

After the Sunday service on January 24, Saji had cycled over to Safdarjung Hospital to visit Paul, who was ill. He said goodbye to his mother at the door, told her he’d be back by 4:30, and rode off. From the hospital, he was heading to a music lesson in Lajpat Nagar.Punalur

Kochumon’s Prompting

Shortly after Saji left, one of the Bethel Bible School students, Kochumon, felt a sudden, strong urge to follow him. He borrowed a classmate’s bicycle and caught up with Saji at Safdarjung Hospital.

“I’m coming with you today,” Kochumon said.

The two of them went together to the music lesson in Lajpat Nagar, which ran from three to four. On the way back to Green Park, near Gulmohar Park, a truck struck them both. They were thrown from their bicycles. Police at the scene found a home address and phone number on Kochumon and called immediately.

The call reached F-58: two boys from this address had been in an accident near Gulmohar Park junction. A Bible school student named Thomas Samuel ran the kilometer to the spot and recognized the bicycles. By then, both Saji and Kochumon had already been rushed to the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) in a police jeep that had been traveling just behind the truck.

Thomas Samuel hadn’t yet returned when a stranger arrived at the gate on a scooter. He looked at an address written on his hand, then turned to Marykutty, who was standing at the gate in anguish.

“I heard police say that two children from this house were in an accident and were being taken to AIIMS. Who are they to you, sister?”

When she said one of them was her son, the man told her to get on the scooter. He took her to the hospital himself. When she tried to find out who he was, he brushed the question aside and left. No one ever learned his name. But because of him, Marykutty reached the hospital in time to see both boys while they were still conscious. It was a small mercy in an unbearable hour.

At the emergency ward, Saji received immediate attention. There were no visible wounds. But inside, he had been devastated by the impact.

Saji’s blood type was O negative, which was expensive and rare. The previous evening, January 23, after the prayer meeting, Brother P. C. Kuriakose from the IIT quarters came to Marykutty and pressed more than two thousand rupees into her hands. “Use it if you need it,” he said. “If not, give it to the pastor when he gets back.” It covered the blood and everything else the hospital required that night.

Saji was taken into surgery without delay and given oxygen and blood transfusions. None of it was enough.

Saji was gone.

A Vision, Three Days Earlier

Three days before the accident, on Thursday evening, January 21, Pastor K. K. Thomas had been in Delhi to teach at the Bible school. That night he saw a vision: two people cycling, a collision, one of them dying, a funeral service. He could not make out who they were.

The next morning, Friday, he mentioned it to Marykutty. She went quiet. She had seen the same dream the night before.

For the rest of that Friday, through Saturday and Sunday, she moved through the days with a dread she couldn’t shake. Something was coming. She didn’t know what.

Saji’s Final Days

Those who knew Saji in the days before his death have remarked on something they noticed but couldn’t quite name at the time. He seemed more present, more deliberate. He moved through his days with a quiet intensity, speaking to teachers and friends at college about faith, handing out gospel tracts, saying goodbyes that no one understood were goodbyes. On the Monday after his death, they found his school bag ready by the door, books packed alongside a fresh stack of gospel tracts for the week ahead.

His musical gifts had been shining with particular brightness in those final weeks. At the Sunday service on the day of the accident, people who were there remember noticing something in his face while he played and sang, a quality they couldn’t name then and still struggle to describe.

The Sunday before his death, he had sung at the service about the hope of the saints, a song whose words pointed toward the world to come. Its refrain was later carved into his gravestone:

What a day that will be
When my Jesus I shall see
When I look upon His face

The one who saved me by His grace
Then He’ll take me by the hand

He’ll lead me through the promised land
What a day, glorious day that will be

The Hope Of God’s Children

Jesus said to her [Martha], “I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die” (John 11:25-26).

Death, for the believer, is not the end of the story. A philosopher once wrote that death does nothing more than pour oil into a lamp that will never go out. Scripture calls death ‘gain’ (Philippians 1:21) and ‘rest’ (Revelation 14:13). At death, the body formed from dust returns to dust, and the spirit returns to the God who gave it (Ecclesiastes 12:7). What sin cost a person in this life, heaven restores in full, and the believer’s personality finds its complete and perfect expression in the presence of God.

The body the saints receive in heaven, Scripture tells us, will be suited for heaven itself: imperishable, glorious, powerful, spiritual, like Christ’s own body, incorruptible and immortal, able to recognize and be recognized, free to move without limitation, both tangible and transcendent. Death, for a child of God, is not loss. It is gain.

For those who live without God, death strips away everything (Psalm 49:16-17). They leave this world with nothing. But for God’s children, death is the journey home, where every act of faithfulness will meet its reward.

The Funeral

On Monday, January 25, Thomas and his cousin arrived in Delhi. Pastor K. K. Thomas and several other ministers and believers had made their way to the Mumbai airport just to meet him on his layover and offer what comfort they could. More were waiting at the Faith Home when he arrived.

The congregation gathered around Thomas and his family. They sang, they prayed, they wept alongside them. The peace of God that passes all understanding is the inheritance of His children even in the most brutal seasons of grief.

On Tuesday afternoon, January 26, Saji’s body was brought from the hospital morgue to the Faith Home. The funeral service was led by Pastor M. K. Chacko, in the presence of a large gathering, ministers and believers from congregations across Delhi, people from different churches and different languages and different faiths, all drawn together by grief and love. One after another, people stood to speak of Saji, their voices breaking, their eyes full. What they said of him was what his life had already said.

The procession to the Christian Cemetery on Prithviraj Road was long. The graveside service was conducted with the same dignity and care. Above the place where they laid him, they inscribed the refrain of the song he had loved best.

Kochumon, who had been with him in the accident, survived without serious injury.

In the weeks that followed, Thomas returned to ministry. He traveled to Kanpur in Uttar Pradesh, then on to Baroda, Ankleshwar, and other parts of Gujarat. He kept going. There was nothing else to do.

A Few Words from Pastor K. V. Abraham

I am delighted that the IPCNR PYPA is publishing the biography of Pastor K. T. Thomas. More than twenty-five years have passed since I first came to know Pastor Thomas and began serving the Lord alongside him. It is therefore a great privilege for me to share a few thoughts about him and the IPC Northern Region.

I first met Pastor K. T. Thomas in 1964. At that time, Pastor N. Stephens and I were serving in the church at Gandhidham while also holding secular jobs. Following a period of significant spiritual revival in 1962, a Pentecostal movement emerged within our congregation. As the church continued to grow, we felt it would be best to place the ministry under the leadership of a full-time servant of God.

In 1964, Pastor K. T. Thomas had already begun a ministry in Jamnagar, Gujarat. We invited him to oversee the Gandhidham church as well and entrusted its ministry to his care. For about a year, he faithfully served both the Jamnagar and Gandhidham congregations. At the end of 1965, he returned to Delhi. Thereafter, Pastor P. S. Thomas and I continued the ministry in Gandhidham, while Pastor K. T. Thomas remained in contact and visited us from time to time.

For many years, I had a desire to leave my government job and enter full-time ministry with the Indian Pentecostal Church of God. Knowing this, Pastor Thomas often spoke to me about the possibility of working together. He also shared his vision of bringing together Pentecostal workers across North India and organizing the work in a more unified manner. I told him that I believed the Indian Pentecostal Church of God was the strongest Pentecostal fellowship in India and that if this work could function as part of the IPC, I would gladly join.

In 1969, during the Delhi Pentecostal Assembly Convention, Pastor Thomas organized a meeting of workers from an IPC background in the presence of the late Pastor C. K. Daniel. During that meeting, a decision was made to establish the IPC Northern Region as a branch of the IPC. This proposal was communicated to the then IPC President, Pastor K. E. Abraham.

Thus, the IPC Northern Region came into existence with Pastor K. V. Kurien as President, Pastor C. C. George as Vice President, and Pastor K. T. Thomas serving as Secretary-Treasurer. After I resigned from my government position in 1971, I assumed the office of Secretary. Later, when Pastor K. V. Kurien moved to New York, Pastor K. T. Thomas became President. By the abundant grace of God, we continue to carry forward this ministry today.

Although the Northern Region began in a very humble way, it has grown to include approximately 160 churches and numerous ministers serving in different languages across North India.

Even before the formation of the Northern Region, many IPC ministers had come to North India to preach the gospel. Some eventually returned home, some continued as independent ministers, and others worked without meaningful fellowship or cooperation with one another or with the IPC. The formation of the IPC Northern Region gave the IPC a recognized presence and identity throughout North India. Much of the credit for this development belongs to the vision, sacrifice, perseverance, and leadership of Pastor K. T. Thomas.

In the early days of the Northern Region, one prominent Pentecostal leader attempted to discourage Pastor Thomas. He argued that the IPC would never take root in North India and suggested that, since many respected leaders had already failed in that effort, Pastor Thomas should abandon the idea as well. Yet despite such opposition and discouragement, Pastor Thomas remained steadfast in his vision and calling. By the grace of God, the work continued to grow and prosper.

This growth was also made possible through the prayerful support of many respected IPC leaders, both those still living and those who have since gone home to be with the Lord. Churches and individual supporters in India and abroad also provided much-needed financial assistance. It is my hope that all who love the Lord will continue to support the ministry of the Northern Region in whatever ways they can.

The ministers and churches of North India have also played a vital role through their wholehearted cooperation. The contributions of Pastor K. V. Kurien, Pastor P. S. Thomas, Pastor C. D. Samuel, Pastor P. A. George, and many others deserve special recognition. The sudden passing of Pastor K. K. Thomas was a particularly painful loss to the work of the Region.

Pastor K. T. Thomas is a man of courage and decisive action. He is an effective leader and administrator. His commitment to doing things properly and with excellence is worthy of imitation. He is also deeply passionate about music and has a special gift for encouraging and training young people to use their talents for the glory of God.

The blessings promised in God’s Word to those who fear Him have been clearly evident in Pastor Thomas’s life. God has blessed him with a devoted life partner, and his children are God-fearing, loving, and gifted in many ways. Under his guidance and encouragement, their musical talents have been a blessing to the ministry of the Northern Region. The love, care, and hospitality shown by this family are well known. Over the years, hundreds of people have enjoyed the privilege of experiencing their generous hospitality firsthand.

Pastor Thomas has endured many hardships and sorrows throughout his life. The untimely death of his son left a deep wound in his heart. Several years ago, he suffered a serious illness that left one side of his body weakened. Yet he continues to serve the Lord with all his strength and determination.

My prayer is that the Lord will grant him many more years of life, health, and strength, and continue to use him powerfully for His glory in the years ahead.

B-199, Apna Nagar, Gopalpuri

A Son’s Tribute

If I were to choose one person from the Bible who most resembles my father, it would be the Apostle Peter. The more I study Peter’s life, the more similarities I see. Their boldness, enthusiasm, leadership, and unwavering dedication to the work of the Lord stand out to me. God used my father to pioneer Christian ministry in the rugged regions of Northern India. At times, his sincerity and good intentions were misunderstood and brought him criticism and hardship. Yet he never compromised his convictions or abandoned his principles. His powerful preaching, deep faith in God, passion for justice, compassion for those in need, and even his well-known temper have made him a memorable figure in India’s Pentecostal community.

My strongest memory of my father is his devotion to the Lord and to the ministry. Although he was raised in a Christian home, there was a time when he turned away from God and became interested in Communism. But from the day he committed his life to Christ, he never looked back. He became a fearless witness for the Lord. On several occasions, extremist Hindus threatened his life, yet he continued to share his faith boldly. There were also people both inside and outside the church who tried to divide congregations, damage the ministry, and publicly accuse him of things he had never done. Our family endured poverty, serious illness, war, and even the loss of my brother Benjamin. Through it all, my father never wavered in his commitment to the Lord.

His dedication to ministry has always inspired and challenged me. From the day God gave him a vision for North India, his life has revolved around building and strengthening that work. For more than three decades, he traveled thousands of miles across India, planting churches and encouraging young ministers. I remember him being away for three or four months at a time. A telegram would arrive from a minister in a distant state asking him to come immediately to help resolve a church crisis or deal with local authorities. He would ask us to roll up and tie his bedding, my mother would quickly prepare food for the journey, and he would leave on the next available train—often without a reservation. Many nights were spent sitting upright with little or no sleep.

He was not paid for this work, nor was he supported by a mission board. At times, he used money that our family needed in order to travel or to help others who were struggling. What kept him going was a deep sense of calling and commitment to the ministry.

Over those same decades, hundreds of church members emigrated to America and the Middle East. Some were key leaders and faithful supporters who had been the backbone of the church. I believe some of my father’s greatest disappointments came when coworkers and leaders he had trained chose to leave India and settle abroad. Such losses would have discouraged many people, but he continued to press on. His heart remained firmly committed to the work in North India.

Of course, he visited America and other countries many times to see former church members and leaders. On numerous occasions, he was invited to pastor churches in America and settle there permanently. Yet his calling remained in North India. I am proud that my father never gave up and never allowed himself to be distracted from that calling. Those who have known him and served alongside him share that same admiration and many of the same memories.

There were times when my father became discouraged and weary. I have seen him exhausted by the burdens of ministry and heard him speak of leaving it altogether. Yet his commitment to the Lord always carried him forward. God has been his source of strength.

As a child, I often woke early in the morning—or even in the middle of the night—and found my father on his knees in prayer. On several occasions, he fasted and prayed for forty days, sustaining himself only on water and juice. After he miraculously recovered from a stroke in 1985 that paralyzed the right side of his body and caused him to lose his speech and memory for three months, my wife Mari and I pleaded with him to retire and come live with us in America. His answer was simple: he was committed to the ministry in North India.

Apart from God, my mother’s support has been one of his greatest sources of strength. Her gentle spirit and calm words have often brought peace during my father’s well-known “moments.” Through every trial, she has stood faithfully by his side, encouraging and supporting him.

Over the years, hundreds of ministers and guests have stayed in our home. It often seemed as though every meal included visitors. My mother faithfully cooked for them and cared for their needs. She is also known for her life of prayer. Not a single day passes without her praying for her husband, his ministry, and each of her children by name. Even now, whenever I face a need, I call her from America and ask her to pray. We trust in the power of her prayers. I thank God for giving me such godly and loving parents.

I am also grateful to Pastor Geevarghese for undertaking the task of writing this biography of my father. He has been a faithful prayer partner and dedicated coworker in the ministry in North India. I believe this book will serve as an excellent resource for young ministers beginning their journey of service and will encourage and strengthen those already engaged in the work of the Lord.

  Stanley Thomas

 

A Tribute from a Younger Colleague

When I came to Delhi in 1987, I wanted to write a brief biography of Pastor K. T. Thomas. For various reasons, however, I never pursued the project. I am delighted to see that this task has now been accomplished through the dedicated efforts of Pastor K. Y. Geevarghese. As I read through the manuscript, I was deeply moved and inspired by the life of this enthusiastic and dynamic servant of God. I sincerely appreciate Pastor K. Y. Geevarghese for undertaking and successfully completing such a demanding and worthwhile task.

The life of Pastor K. T. Thomas stands as a powerful testimony to God’s grace and sustaining power. The fact that he remains active in ministry today is itself evidence of God’s faithfulness and a reminder that the Lord still has work for him to accomplish through the ministry of IPCNR.

I have known Pastor K. T. Thomas personally since 1979. Throughout these years, I have held him in high regard as a genuine servant of God, wholly devoted to the Lord’s work through IPC. He is compassionate toward the poor and needy, sincere in heart, and unwavering in his convictions. He is not one to compromise when it comes to doctrinal truth or matters of principle. I have always admired his courage to speak the truth plainly, regardless of the consequences. At times, however, his frankness and directness have made him the target of strong criticism.

Pastor K. T. Thomas is also a gifted musician and songwriter. Anyone who has attended a meeting or convention organized by him can easily recognize his passion for music. The musical choir known as the Zion Singers, which ministers at major IPCNR conventions in the capital city, owes much of its existence and growth to his vision and encouragement. His songs are especially meaningful because many of them were born out of personal trials and deep spiritual experiences. Pastor K. T. Thomas is known for his kindness and hospitality, even toward strangers. He has a deep concern for fellow workers serving in remote areas and is sensitive to their needs. One incident illustrates this well. On one occasion, when I visited him at F-58, Green Park, New Delhi—which served both as his residence and the IPCNR office—I found him deeply concerned because financial support for two unmarried evangelists serving in Nagaland had been delayed. Although I was in a hurry to attend to an urgent matter elsewhere, he persuaded me to help send their support immediately through a Telegram Money Order from the main post office in Delhi.

Another admirable quality of Pastor K. T. Thomas is his desire to see people recognized and placed where they can best serve according to their gifts and calling. His life is both a challenge and an example to all who sincerely seek to serve the Lord. It is my prayer that God will grant him many more years of health and strength as he continues to carry the light of the gospel throughout the rugged and often difficult regions of North India.

  Evg. Sunny George
Former Joint Secretary, PYPA NR &
Former President, CDPYPA, Delhi
