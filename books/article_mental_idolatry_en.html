What Is Mental Idolatry?

What Is Mental Idolatry?

Alex M. Kuruvilla

“Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry.” (1Corinthians 10:14)

‘Flee from idolatry’ is a command given by the apostle Paul to the church in Corinth, which applies not only to that congregation but to every visible church everywhere. Any local church that claims to be truly apostolic must take this doctrine seriously. Why? Because idolatry – whether expressed through physical objects or formed within the mind – is nothing less than spiritual adultery. It is a grave offense against the holy and living God.

Scripture defines idolatry as the exchange of the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal beings – man, birds, animals, and creeping things (Romans 1:23). At its core, idolatry is the worship of the creature rather than the Creator (Romans 1:25). True monotheistic faith maintains a clear distinction between Creator and creation. Pantheistic systems, however, blur this distinction by claiming that the Creator and the creation are essentially one. In philosophical terms, this is expressed in the concept of Advaita (‘not two’). Within such a worldview, there is no restraint against elevating elements of nature to the status of deity and worshipping them as God.

The God of the Bible is not an abstract force; He is relational. He enters into a covenant with His people and calls for mutual faithfulness. When that covenant is violated, His response is not indifference but righteous jealousy – like that of a husband whose beloved has been unfaithful. When God’s chosen people turn to idols, it is not merely an error of judgment; it is covenantal unfaithfulness – spiritual adultery. Idolatry provokes this divine jealousy because it replaces Him with something false.

What, then, happens in idolatry? God alone possesses incommunicable attributes–qualities that cannot be shared with any created being. He declares, 'I am the LORD (YAHWEH), that is My name; And My glory I will not give to another, Nor My praise to carved images' (Isaiah 42:8). His glory – His very identity as God - belongs to Him alone. Indeed, the Bible repeatedly affirms His incomparability:

‘To whom then will you liken God? Or what likeness will you compare to Him?’ (Isaiah 40:18).

‘To whom then will you liken Me, Or to whom shall I be equal? says the Holy One’ (Isaiah 40:25).

There is absolutely nothing in all creation that can be compared with God.

God’s incommunicable attributes include His 1) Sovereignty, 2) Omnipotence, 3) Omniscience, 4) Omnipresence, 5) Transcendence, 6) Immanence, 7) Immutability, 8) Infinitude, and 9) Eternality. The moment we attempt to represent God through an image—whether physical or mental—we inevitably project these divine attributes onto something created. In doing so, we dethrone the true God and enthrone a substitute. We redefine God according to our own imagination. In essence, we place our own will above His—making ourselves god (‘Aham Bramhasmi’ = ‘I am god’). Idolatry, therefore, is ultimately self-worship.

There is another serious dimension to idolatry: it replaces truth with falsehood. An idol does not merely obscure the truth about God—it actively proclaims a lie about Him. Consider the incident of the golden calf in the wilderness (Exodus 32). The Israelites did not abandon the name of the true God; they misapplied it. They fashioned a calf and declared, “This is your god, O Israel, who brought you out of Egypt!” (Exodus 32:4). In doing so, they reduced the invisible, eternal, and all-powerful God to a visible, perishable, and powerless image. The result was devastating—thousands perished under God’s judgment. It is important to recognize that the golden calf did not originate in metal—it was first conceived in the mind. The physical idol was simply the outward expression of an inward distortion. Idolatry always begins in the heart before it manifests in visible form.

Idolatry is a blatant violation of the first two commandments of the Ten Commandments. They are:

  1. You shall have no other gods before Me (Exodus 20:3).
  2. You shall not make for yourself a carved image—any likeness of anything that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth; you shall not bow down to them nor serve them (Exodus 20:4-5).

Furthermore, the Lord God Yahweh has clearly warned us not to imitate the worship practices of pagans:

‘You must not worship the Lord your God in their way.’ (Deuteronomy 12:4).

‘You must not worship the Lord your God in their way, because in worshiping their gods, they do all kinds of detestable things the Lord hates.’ (Deuteronomy 12:31a).

This brings us to a sobering reality. Many images, statues, and icons claimed to represent Jesus Christ are widely accepted and even venerated within Christian settings. These are placed in churches and homes, and people bow before them, pray before them, and offer rituals in their presence. Yet these images are not the true Christ—they are human creations shaped by imagination. They do not reveal Him as He truly is.

Consider the crucifix. What does it communicate? Does it proclaim the risen and glorified Lord? Or does it present Christ as still hanging on the cross? Instead of revealing the full truth, it risks directing attention to a partial and misleading representation. In this way, it can obscure as much as it reveals.

So, what is mental idolatry? Mental idolatry occurs when such images—whether seen externally or formed internally—are brought into the mind and used as aids in worship or prayer. When we rely on imagined pictures to approach God, we are engaging in a subtle yet serious form of idolatry. Some even recall cinematic portrayals, such as scenes from The Passion, to visualize Christ during worship or the Lord’s Supper. But this, too, is a construct of human imagination. Mental idolatry is not harmless—it is the inward counterpart of physical idolatry. Both arise from the same root: a failure to worship God as He truly is, and a tendency to reshape Him according to human perception.

Therefore, the command remains urgent and uncompromising: flee from idolatry—in every form, whether outward or inward, visible or invisible.
