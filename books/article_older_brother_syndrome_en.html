Older Son Syndrome

The Older Son Syndrome

Alex M. Kuruvilla

What does ‘Older Son Syndrome’ mean?

‘Older Son Syndrome’ is a subtle and dangerous spiritual condition into which even sincere, professing Christians can fall, one that ultimately leads away from the grace of God.

To understand this, we must turn to Luke 15, a chapter that stands as one of the most profound revelations of God’s heart in all Scripture. The content of Chapter 15 is the oracle of our Lord Jesus Christ, the very God from the very God. This chapter is foundational to knowing God. I do not know where I would have been had this chapter not been there in the Bible. If Chapter 15 were likened to a precious necklace, the three parables would be radiant diamonds bound together by a single thread, each reflecting the glory of the triune God in the work of salvation.

The third of these parables is commonly, but inadequately, called the Parable of the Prodigal Son. A more fitting title would be The Parable of the Prodigal Father and his Lost Sons. The true focus is not the younger son’s rebellion but the father’s extravagant love. He is the central figure—the one who runs, embraces, restores, and celebrates. He represents God the Father in His abundant mercy. The second important character is the older son, the villain, whose background highlights the father’s magnificent character. He stands for the self-righteous, spiritually proud, like the Pharisee. And it is here that we encounter the essence of “Older Son Syndrome.” The younger son represents each one of us, open sinners.

When the younger son returns home in repentance, the father rejoices and throws a great feast. This celebration mirrors the joy of heaven over one sinner who repents. The older son was in the field. He learned from a servant that the party was about his younger brother, whom he had written off a long time ago. The older son was furious with his father’s actions: he believed his father was honoring the undeserving while failing to recognize the deserving. As a result, he refused to join the celebration. His father came out and begged him to enter. But he responded, saying,

‘Look! All these years, I’ve been slaving for you and never disobeyed your orders. Yet you never gave me even a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours who has squandered your property with prostitutes comes home, you kill the fattened calf for him!’ (Luke 15:29-30).

And the father said to him, ‘My son, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours. But we had to celebrate and be glad, because this brother of yours was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found.’ (Luke 15:31-32).

The older son’s response exposes him. As for him, a young goat seems to be far more valuable than his lost brother. He never left his father. He lived with him under one roof. Yet, he had no intimate personal knowledge of his father. He did not know how gracious, how loving, and how good his father was.

Whereas the younger son, though he rebelled, knew his father well; he knew how good his father was and that his father would forgive him if he asked. He affirmed his understanding of his father in these words:

“How many of my father’s hired servants have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say to him, ‘Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you, and I am no longer worthy to be called your son. Make me like one of your hired servants.’” (Luke 15:17-19).

In fact, it is this knowledge that prompted the younger son to return to his father. His return was not based on worthiness, but on trust in his father’s compassion.

As for the older son, he considered himself a slave to his father, toiling against his will to please his father. His motive for obeying did not originate in love for his father. Like a slave, he was striving hard to earn his merit to get favors (gifts) from his father. Because of this slave mentality, he never experienced the sweet fellowship with his Father. In fact, he did not love his father at all. For had he loved his father, he would have surely loved his brother. The sacred scripture attests this truth. We read:

‘If someone says, “I love God,” and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God whom he has not seen? And this commandment we have from Him: that he who loves God must love his brother also.’ (I John 4:20-21).

The father’s reply, ‘My son, you are always with me, and everything I have is yours,’ implies a piercing question: If everything was already yours, why did you never enjoy it? The answer is clear; he was trying to earn what could only be received as a gift. His obedience and labor were driven by a desire to earn his father’s reward.

This parable reveals a profound truth about God. Because God is an absolute sovereign and self-sufficient entity, you cannot obligate Him to repay. People fast not to humble themselves (or afflict their souls), but to get things from God; they tithe, expecting tenfold in return.

God gives gifts; obviously, gifts are free. The only manner in which you can receive a gift from God is by stretching out your hands in faith. The absolute sovereign God never repays. Sacred scripture attests this truth. The LORD said to Job: “Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine.”' (Job 41:11).

“For who has known the mind of the Lord?
Or who has become His counselor?
“Or who has first given to Him,
and it shall be repaid to him?”
For of Him and through Him and
to Him are all things,
to whom be glory forever. Amen.’
(Romans 11:34-36).

Incidentally, there is one thing that God repays. The Lord says, “Vengeance is mine, I will repay” (Romans 12:19).

God is no man’s debtor. But we are misapplying this truth of God. By fasting, tithing, etc., we are trying to make Him a debtor to receive from Him manyfold. In the Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20), the laborers hired early in the morning attempted to receive more than the agreed wages by putting the master of the vineyard in a debt position. But the master did not yield to their manipulation. Similarly, salvation comes to us free of any works. We read:

‘For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.’ (Ephesians 2:8-9).

“Older Son Syndrome,” then, is not merely about attitude; it is about a fundamental misunderstanding of grace. It is the tragedy of being near to God, yet never truly knowing Him.
